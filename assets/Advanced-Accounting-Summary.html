Chapter 1 — Introduction to Accounting Standards

Source: 74685bos60485-inter-p1-cp1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • GAAP: Generally Accepted Accounting Principles are the common set of accepted principles, standards and procedures a reporting entity must follow when preparing and presenting financial statements. Internationally these are IFRS; in India they are Accounting Standards (AS) and Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS).
  • Introduction to AS: AS are written policy documents issued by Government (MCA, in consultation with NFRA) dealing with four aspects of transactions/events: recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure. Their purpose is timely, useful financial information to investors and stakeholders.
  • Benefits of AS: (i) standardisation of alternative accounting treatments; (ii) requirements for additional disclosures; (iii) comparability of financial statements (intra-enterprise and inter-enterprise); plus reduction of scope for creative accounting.
  • AS reduce accounting alternatives within bounds of rationality. Being principle-based, application is judgemental for complex transactions; in any conflict, Statute prevails over AS.
  • How many AS: ICAI has issued 29 AS. AS 6 (Depreciation Accounting) was withdrawn on revision of AS 10, and AS 8 (Accounting for R&D) withdrawn on issuance of AS 26 — effectively 27 AS at present, notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021.

Key Definitions / Terms

Accounting Standards (AS)
Written policy documents issued by Government/MCA (in consultation with NFRA) on recognition, measurement, presentation & disclosure of transactions and events.
ASB
Accounting Standards Board, constituted by ICAI in 1977; independent in formulating standards; includes representatives of industry (ASSOCHAM, CII, FICCI), regulators, academicians and government.
IASB / IASC
International Accounting Standards Committee (London, 1973, 75+ countries incl. ICAI) restructured into the International Accounting Standards Board (effective 1 April 2001).
IFRS
Comprises IFRS issued by IASB, IAS issued by IASC, and interpretations by SIC and IFRIC; a principles-based set of standards. Standards issued up to 31.03.2001 are IAS; from 01.04.2001 are IFRS.
Ind AS
IFRS-converged standards issued by the Central Government under supervision of ASB of ICAI and in consultation with NFRA.
Carve-out / Carve-in
Carve-out = deviation from IFRS made for India's economic environment; Carve-in = additional guidance given in Ind AS over and above IFRS.
Convergence vs Adoption
Adoption = use IFRS as issued by IASB on a set timetable; Convergence = develop compatible high-quality standards aligned with IFRS, fully or partially. India chose convergence.

How It Works / Logic

  • Standard-setting process (ASB): identify area → constitute study groups → prepare & circulate draft (to ICAI Council and outside bodies: MCA, SEBI, C&AG, CBDT, SCOPE) → ascertain views of those bodies → finalise exposure draft (invite public comments) → consider comments → modify draft (Council + ASB) → issue AS (ICAI for non-corporates; MCA in consultation with NFRA for corporates).
  • Earlier ASB issued Accounting Standard Interpretations (ASIs); their consensus portions have since been merged as 'Explanation' into the relevant AS to harmonise the ICAI and MCA sets.
  • Per Section 133, Companies Act 2013, the Central Government prescribes standards as recommended by ICAI after examining NFRA recommendations.
  • Need for convergence to global standards: (1) raising funds from international markets; (2) comparability of financial statements for cross-border filings; (3) uniformity, rationalisation, transparency and adaptability; (4) global investment (free flow of capital, lower risk of misjudgement, no costly restatement).
  • Becoming IFRS compliant: Technique I — Adoption; Technique II — Convergence. India converges via Ind AS, numbered by adding 100 to the IFRS number (IFRS 1 → Ind AS 101); Ind AS corresponding to IAS keep the same number. Two sets coexist: Ind AS (IFRS-converged) and existing notified AS.
  • Formulation of Ind AS departures: terminology differences (e.g., "statement of profit and loss"); removal of options (not carve-outs); differences in economic environment (carve-outs).

Examples

  • Standardisation of alternatives (disclosure of cost formula): Suppose two similar traders each hold the same closing inventory but one values it on FIFO and the other on Weighted Average, giving different closing stock and hence different profits. AS 2 does not force a single formula, but it does require each enterprise to disclose the cost formula actually used (FIFO, Weighted Average, etc.). A user reading the notes therefore knows which of the permitted alternatives produced the reported figure and can adjust for comparison — this is exactly the "standardisation of alternative treatments + additional disclosure" benefit at work.
  • Creative accounting curbed: Imagine a company spends ₹10,00,000 on ordinary staff training (a revenue expense). By wrongly capitalising it as an "intangible asset", profit and assets are both overstated by ₹10,00,000 in the current year. Conversely, writing off a genuine ₹10,00,000 machine (capital expenditure) straight to the profit and loss account would understate both profit and assets. Recognition and measurement rules in the AS close off both manoeuvres, reducing the scope for such creative accounting.
  • Ind AS numbering rule (convergence in practice): When an IFRS is converged into an Indian standard, 100 is added to the IFRS number — so IFRS 1 becomes Ind AS 101 and IFRS 15 becomes Ind AS 115. Standards that correspond to an older IAS keep the same number (IAS 2 → Ind AS 2). This lets a reader instantly map an Ind AS back to its IFRS/IAS source.
  • Carve-out vs terminology change: Renaming the "statement of comprehensive income" as the statement of profit and loss, and the "statement of financial position" as the balance sheet, is only a terminology change to match Indian law — it is not a carve-out because it does not deviate from any IFRS accounting principle. A true carve-out is a substantive deviation made for India's economic environment; additional guidance inserted over and above IFRS is a carve-in.

Why It Matters / Application

Standards improve credibility and comparability (intra- and inter-enterprise), raise investor confidence, and enable cross-border capital flow and global listing. The ICAI (as an IFAC member) converges with IFRS to formulate globally accepted financial statements; convergence eases group consolidation and acceptance of Indian companies' statements on foreign stock exchanges.

Note

History & roadmap: ICAI began the IFRS move in 2006; MCA (Companies (Indian Accounting Standards) Rules, 2015) made Ind AS voluntary from 1 April 2015 and mandatory from 1 April 2016 (listed/unlisted with net worth ₹500 crore+); Phase II from 1 April 2017 (net worth ₹250–500 crore). Separate roadmaps: NBFCs from 1 April 2018 (₹500 crore+) and 1 April 2019 (₹250–500 crore); voluntary adoption not allowed for NBFCs. Scheduled Commercial Banks (excl. RRBs) deferred by RBI until further notice; UCBs and RRBs exempt; Insurers deferred by IRDAI until further notice. Companies on SME exchanges are exempt, and once Ind AS is adopted there is no going back.

Chapter 2 — Framework for Preparation & Presentation of Financial Statements

Source: 74686bos60485-inter-p1-cp2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • The ASB Framework (issued July 2000) provides the fundamental basis for developing new standards and reviewing existing ones, and for topics not yet covered by any AS. It is relevant in the context of the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021.
  • Principal areas covered: components of financial statements; objectives; assumptions underlying FS; qualitative characteristics; elements; recognition criteria; measurement principles; and concepts of capital and capital maintenance.
  • Purpose: to assist enterprises in preparing FS, ASB in developing/reviewing and harmonising AS, auditors in forming opinions, users in interpretation, and others interested in ASB's work.
  • Status & scope: applies to general-purpose FS (annual, external users) of all commercial/industrial/business enterprises, public or private. Special-purpose reports (e.g., tax computations) are outside scope. The Framework does not override any AS — in conflict, the AS prevails.

Key Definitions / Terms

Asset
Resource controlled by the enterprise as a result of past events, from which future economic benefits are expected to flow (need not have physical substance; control matters more than legal ownership).
Liability
Present obligation from past events, whose settlement is expected to result in outflow of resources embodying economic benefits.
Equity
Residual interest in the assets after deducting all liabilities; owners' claim (capital and reserves), i.e., A − L = E.
Income
Increase in economic benefits (inflows/asset enhancement or liability decrease) raising equity, other than contributions from equity participants; encompasses revenue and gains.
Expense
Decrease in economic benefits (outflows/asset depletion or liability incurrence) reducing equity, other than distributions to participants; encompasses expenses and losses.
Measurement
Process of determining the money value at which an element is recognised in the balance sheet or statement of profit and loss.

How It Works / Logic

  • Components of FS: Balance Sheet (economic resources, liquidity, solvency), Statement of Profit and Loss (performance/profitability), Cash Flow Statement (generation/use of cash), and Notes/other statements (supplementary information — accounting policies, segment, related party, EPS, etc.). All are interrelated.
  • Objectives & users: to provide information on financial position, performance and cash flows useful for economic decisions. Seven user groups: investors, employees, lenders, suppliers/other trade creditors, customers, governments & agencies, and public.
  • Fundamental accounting assumptions: Going Concern, Accrual (mandatory for companies under Section 128(1)), Consistency — assumed followed unless a departure is disclosed.
  • Qualitative characteristics: Understandability; Relevance (judged by materiality — a threshold, not a primary characteristic); Reliability (faithful representation, substance over form, neutrality, prudence, completeness); Comparability (inter-firm and intra-firm). Constraints: timeliness and balance between benefit and cost.
  • True and fair view: not dealt with directly, but applying the qualitative characteristics and appropriate AS normally results in a true and fair view.
  • Elements: asset, liability, equity, income, expense (gains/losses are not separate elements — they share the economic characteristics of income/expenses). Recognition of income needs a corresponding asset increase or liability decrease; expenses recognised by matching or systematic allocation (e.g., depreciation). Profit = (CA − CL) − (OA − OL) − C + D.
  • Measurement bases (four): Historical Cost (acquisition price), Current Cost (undiscounted current replacement/settlement amount), Realisable (Settlement) Value, and Present Value (discounted future net cash flows). AS largely use historical cost, with exceptions (e.g., inventory at lower of cost and NRV).

Examples

  • Going concern vs break-up basis (Illustration 1): A trader's balance sheet at 31.03.20X1 shows Capital ₹60,000, P&L ₹25,000, 10% Loan ₹35,000, Trade payables ₹10,000; and PPE ₹65,000, Stock ₹30,000, Trade receivables ₹20,000, Deferred expenditure ₹10,000, Bank ₹5,000. The same facts for the next year produce very different statements depending on whether the going-concern assumption holds:
    ItemCase (i) — Going concernCase (ii) — Not a going concern
    PPE (5-yr life, even use; NRV ₹60,000)₹52,000 (cost ₹65,000 less ₹13,000 depreciation)₹60,000 (net realisable value)
    Stock (cost ₹32,000; NRV ₹40,000)₹32,000 (lower of cost and NRV)₹40,000 (realisable value)
    Deferred expenditure (₹10,000, amortise over 4 yrs)₹7,500 (₹2,500 amortised)Nil (fully written off — no future benefit)
    Trade receivables (₹25,000; ₹2,000 doubtful; ₹4,000 depends on re-installation)₹23,000 (less ₹2,000 provision)₹19,000 (also drop the uncertain ₹4,000)
    10% Loan (₹2,500 early-repayment penalty)₹35,000 (penalty ignored — not repaying early)₹37,500 (penalty payable on winding up)
    Trade payables (₹12,000, settle at 5% discount)₹12,000₹11,400 (net of ₹600 discount)
    Resulting P&L balance₹44,600₹47,200
    The lesson: going concern lets you carry assets at cost/amortised amounts and ignore break-up costs; abandoning it forces realisable values and recognition of settlement penalties.
  • Liability recognition (Example 2): A Ltd has a binding agreement with P Ltd to buy a custom-made machine for ₹40,000. Before delivery, A Ltd changes its production method and the machine becomes useless, with no resale value. Because a present obligation exists from a past event (the binding order), A Ltd must recognise a ₹40,000 liability to P Ltd; and since the machine will yield no future economic benefit, the ₹40,000 is charged to the profit and loss account as a "Loss on change in production method". Entry: Dr Loss on change in production method ₹40,000 / Cr P Ltd ₹40,000.
  • Present value & recoverable value (Example 7): A machine has a carrying amount of ₹40,000 (cost less depreciation). Its net selling price today is ₹35,000, and it can generate ₹10,000 net cash inflow for each of the next 5 years. Discounting at the PV factors 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683 and 0.621:
    1. Value in use = ₹10,000 × (0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 + 0.683 + 0.621) = ₹10,000 × 3.79 = ₹37,900.
    2. Recoverable value = higher of net selling price (₹35,000) and value in use (₹37,900) = ₹37,900.
    3. Carrying amount ₹40,000 exceeds recoverable value ₹37,900, so the asset is overstated by ₹2,100 and must be written down.
    This illustrates the Present Value measurement basis and why an asset cannot be carried above what it can recover.
  • Capital maintenance under all three bases (Example 8–9, Illustration 2): A trader starts 20X1 with ₹12,000 = 6,000 units at ₹2, sells them at ₹3 (cash ₹18,000) and withdraws ₹6,000, leaving ₹12,000 cash. Whether capital is "maintained" depends on the basis:
    BasisOpening capital to maintainClosing equityRetained profit
    Financial capital at historical cost₹12,000₹12,000Nil — capital maintained
    Financial capital at current purchasing power (average index 100 → 120)₹12,000 × 120/100 = ₹14,400₹12,000(–) ₹2,400 — drawings should have been ₹3,600, not ₹6,000
    Physical capital at current cost (specific index 100 → 125, i.e. ₹2 → ₹2.50)6,000 × ₹2.50 = ₹15,000₹12,000(–) ₹3,000 — drawings should have been ₹3,000
    Under both current-value bases the ₹12,000 left is not enough to re-buy 6,000 units, so part of the ₹6,000 drawn was really capital, not profit.

Why It Matters / Application

Financial statements must show a true and fair view; applying the qualitative characteristics plus appropriate AS normally achieves this. Capital (net assets) maintenance ensures dividends do not erode capital — the Companies Act bars dividend without providing depreciation. Maintenance is tested via three bases: Financial capital at historical cost, Financial capital at current purchasing power (average price indices), and Physical capital at current cost (specific price indices per asset).

Note

Reported profit depends on the valuation basis of assets and liabilities; since historical cost is mostly used, reported profits are largely historical-cost based. Retained Profit = (CA − CL) − (OA − OL) − C must not be negative for capital to be maintained.

Chapter 3 — Applicability of Accounting Standards

Source: 74687bos60485-inter-p1-cp3.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Status of AS: developed by ASB of ICAI, approved/notified by MCA for corporates; they cannot override laws and local regulations but are mandatory from notified dates and generally apply to all enterprises subject to exceptions.
  • Three applicability questions: does the standard apply (a) to the enterprise concerned? (b) to the financial statement concerned? (c) to the financial item concerned?
  • Enterprises covered: any enterprise (corporate, co-operative or other) engaged in commercial/industrial/business activity, whether or not profit-oriented, even if charitable/religious. Excluded only if no part of activity is commercial/industrial/business in nature.
  • Financial items: AS apply only to material items — materiality judged by information content, not size (e.g., a ₹50,000 penalty is material for its information even for a large company).

Key Definitions / Terms

SMC
Small and Medium-Sized Company (Companies (AS) Rules, 2021): unlisted (nor in process of listing); not a bank/FI/insurer; turnover (excl. other income) ≤ ₹250 crore; borrowings (incl. public deposits) ≤ ₹50 crore; and not a holding/subsidiary of a non-SMC.
Non-SMC
Company not meeting SMC criteria; must comply with all AS in their entirety.
MSME (non-company)
Level II, III and IV non-company entities (medium, small, micro size); the terms 'SME'/'Small and Medium Enterprise' are read as 'MSME'.
ICDS
Income Computation and Disclosure Standards — 10 standards notified under Section 145(2) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, for computing "Profits and gains of business or profession" / "Income from other sources" (from A.Y. 2017-18) by assessees on the mercantile system.

How It Works / Logic

  • Implication of mandatory status: where the governing statute does not require compliance (e.g., a partnership firm), members of ICAI must, in their attest function, examine compliance and disclose any deviation. Responsibility for preparation rests with management; the auditor forms and reports an opinion.
  • Companies Act provisions: Section 129(1) requires FS per AS notified under Section 133; Section 143(3)(e) requires the auditor to report compliance; Section 129(5) requires disclosure of any deviation, its reasons and financial effects. Carve-outs for non-disclosure exist for insurance, banking and electricity companies and companies governed by other laws.
  • AS vs Income Tax: an expense charged per AS is not necessarily tax-deductible (fiscal policy differs) — e.g., depreciation on a finance-leased asset is charged to the lessee under AS 19 but allowed to the lessor for tax; revenue recognition cannot be avoided merely because exempt under Section 10.
  • Non-company entities — four levels (effective 1 April 2020): Level I large, Level II medium, Level III small, Level IV micro. Level I complies fully; Levels II–IV get exemptions/relaxations and must disclose being an MSME and its level.
  • Level criteria: Level I — listed/in process; banks/FIs/insurers; turnover > ₹250 crore; borrowings > ₹50 crore; or holding/subsidiary of these. Level II — turnover ₹50–250 crore or borrowings ₹10–50 crore. Level III — turnover ₹10–50 crore or borrowings ₹2–10 crore. Level IV — all others.
  • Companies — two levels: SMC and Non-SMC. AS 17 (Segment Reporting) is not applicable to SMCs in entirety; relaxations given for AS 15 (Employee Benefits), AS 19 (Leases), AS 20 (diluted EPS), AS 28 (value in use) and AS 29 (disclosures). AS 25 applies only if the company is required/elects to prepare interim reports.
  • Additional requirements: once an entity ceases to qualify it must comply from the current period (previous figures need not be revised); an entity moving down from Level I/SMC gets relaxations only after two consecutive years out of the higher category; partial exemptions must not mislead.

Examples

  • Classifying a non-company entity — M/s Omega & Co (Example 1): Omega & Co is a partnership firm with turnover (excluding other income) of ₹1.25 crore and borrowings of ₹0.95 crore in the previous year, and wants to avail the AS exemptions for non-corporate entities for FY 20X1.
    1. Test against Level IV criteria: turnover must not exceed ₹10 crore and borrowings must not exceed ₹2 crore. Omega's ₹1.25 crore turnover and ₹0.95 crore borrowings are both below these limits.
    2. It is therefore a Level IV (micro) non-company entity.
    3. Consequence: AS 3, AS 14, AS 17, AS 18, AS 20, AS 21, AS 23, AS 24, AS 25, AS 27 and AS 28 are not applicable to it in their entirety; and relaxations from certain requirements of AS 10, AS 11, AS 13, AS 15, AS 19, AS 22, AS 26 and AS 29 are available. It must disclose that it is an MSME and its level.
  • SMC vs Non-SMC — a company classification: XYZ Ltd is unlisted (and not in the process of listing), is not a bank, financial institution or insurer, has turnover (excluding other income) of ₹50 crore and borrowings including public deposits of ₹1 crore, and is not a holding/subsidiary of a Non-SMC. Checking each SMC condition: turnover ₹50 crore ≤ ₹250 crore ✓, borrowings ₹1 crore ≤ ₹50 crore ✓, unlisted ✓, not a bank/FI/insurer ✓, not tied to a Non-SMC ✓ — so XYZ Ltd qualifies as an SMC. As an SMC it need not apply AS 17 (Segment Reporting) at all and gets relaxations in AS 15, AS 19, AS 20 (diluted EPS), AS 28 and AS 29. Had its turnover been, say, ₹300 crore or its borrowings ₹60 crore, it would fail a single condition and become a Non-SMC, obliged to comply with all AS in full.
  • The two-consecutive-years rule: A company that was previously a Non-SMC (e.g. turnover fell from ₹300 crore to ₹200 crore this year) now meets every SMC test. Even so, it cannot claim SMC exemptions in this first qualifying year — it becomes entitled to them only after it has remained an SMC for two consecutive accounting periods. This prevents entities from flipping status year to year merely to dodge disclosures.

Why It Matters / Application

Classification determines exactly which standards and disclosures an entity must apply, balancing comparability against the cost of compliance for smaller entities. AS improve comparability, transparency and fairness, but AS treatment does not govern tax deductibility — that follows fiscal policy and the 10 ICDS.

Note

The ICAI announcement (400th meeting, 18–19 March 2021) on 'Criteria for Classification of Entities and Applicability of Accounting Standards' governs non-company entities and supersedes the 2008 harmonisation announcement and the 2013 Level-II revision; it is not relevant to non-company entities required to follow Ind AS. AS 21, 23, 25 and 27 do not, by themselves, require a non-company entity to present consolidated or interim statements — they apply only if the entity is required to or elects to prepare them.

Chapter 4 Unit 1 — AS 1: Disclosure of Accounting Policies

Source: 74688bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Diversity in accounting policies is unavoidable: standards cannot cover every area (enterprises may adopt any reasonable policy where none exists), and no single set of policies suits all enterprises for all time. Even within areas a standard covers, more than one policy is often permitted.
  • Because different policies produce different reported figures for the same underlying transactions, comparability suffers. Mere compliance with standards is not enough — AS 1 requires enterprises to disclose the significant accounting policies actually adopted.
  • Purpose: promote better understanding of financial statements, enable meaningful comparison across enterprises for the same period, and (via disclosure of changes) across periods for the same enterprise. AS 1 applies to all enterprises.

Key Definitions / Terms

Accounting Policies
The specific accounting principles and the methods of applying those principles adopted by an enterprise in preparing and presenting financial statements (e.g. inventory: FIFO/Weighted Average; cash flow: Direct/Indirect method). The list of areas is endless.
Going Concern
Assumption that the enterprise will continue operations in the foreseeable future with no intention or need to materially curtail the scale of operations; recognises need to retain profit to replace assets and provide for liabilities.
Consistency
Using the same accounting policies for similar transactions across all accounting periods; improves comparability over time. May be changed only if required by statute, by an accounting standard, or for more appropriate presentation.
Accrual
Transactions recognised as they occur, whether or not cash/cash equivalent is received or paid; gives better matching of revenue and cost. Mandatory for companies under Section 128(1), Companies Act 2013.
Prudence
Profits are not anticipated but losses are provided for; provide for all known liabilities/losses on a best-estimate basis. Ensures profits/assets not overstated and losses/liabilities not understated. Does not permit hidden reserves.
Substance over Form
Account for and present transactions per their substance and financial reality, not merely their legal form.
Materiality
Disclose all items whose knowledge might influence users' decisions; not always a matter of relative size (e.g. a small fraud may signal a serious control flaw).

How It Works / Logic

  • Three Fundamental Accounting Assumptions — Going Concern, Consistency, Accrual: if followed, no specific disclosure is required; if NOT followed, the fact must be disclosed. They are not usually stated because their use is assumed.
  • Accrual basis, being logically superior, is generally followed; but it risks recognising income before receipt, so no revenue is recognised unless consideration and its realisation are reasonably certain. If any income/expense is on a cash basis, that fact should be stated.
  • Selection of policies is governed by Prudence, Substance over Form and Materiality, to portray a true and fair view of performance and state of affairs.
  • Materiality — quantitative limits (Schedule III, Companies Act 2013): (a) disclose in notes any income/expenditure item exceeding 1% of revenue from operations or ₹1,00,000, whichever is higher; (b) disclose each shareholder holding more than 5% of shares, with number held.
  • Manner of disclosure: all significant accounting policies should form part of the financial statements and normally be disclosed in one place (not scattered).
  • Change in accounting policy: disclose any change having a material effect in the current period, plus the amount by which items are affected (to the extent ascertainable); where not ascertainable wholly/partly, indicate the fact. If the change has no current material effect but is reasonably expected to have a material effect in later periods, disclose the fact of change in the period of adoption.

Examples

  • Prudence (Example 1): 500 units bought @₹10; 400 sold @₹15. Value closing 100 units at lower of cost and NRV — if NRV ₹15, value at cost ₹10 (profit ₹2,000); if NRV ₹8, value at ₹8, recognising loss (profit ₹1,800).
  • Prudence (Example 2): no provision for a damages suit unless probability of losing exceeds probability of not losing.
  • Change disclosure (Illustration 1): switch FIFO→weighted average; FIFO ₹1,63,000, WA ₹1,47,000, NRV ₹1,95,000 — disclose method change and effect (profit and inventory reduced by ₹16,000).
  • Illustration 2 (Jagannath Ltd): inventory revalued at works cost ₹50cr (was prime cost ₹30cr) increased profit ₹20cr; ₹10cr provision for permanent diminution reduced profit ₹10cr — both disclosed in notes.
  • Illustration 3 (XYZ financial services): non-provision of overdue interest violates accrual; company must provide the liability — treatment adopted is incorrect.
  • Provision for non-moving inventory shifted from 12-month-issue basis to technical evaluation (₹3.5L→₹2.5L) is a change in estimate, NOT a change in accounting policy; the ₹1L difference is immaterial.

Why It Matters / Application

Disclosure of significant accounting policies and of changes therein is what makes financial statements comparable both across enterprises in the same industry and across periods for one enterprise. Since uniformity is impossible, disclosure lets users adjust their analysis and make informed decisions, mitigating the loss of comparability caused by policy diversity.

Note

Comparability is the qualitative characteristic most affected by policy diversity. A change in the method of estimating a provision is a change in estimate (not policy). Where a change has material effect both currently and in later periods, the fact must be disclosed in the current (adoption) period — not only later.

Chapter 4 Unit 2 — AS 3: Cash Flow Statement

Source: 74689bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • The Cash Flow Statement (CFS) shows the sources from which cash was generated (inflows) and how it was used (outflows) during the period, classified into operating, investing and financing activities.
  • Mandatory for Non-SMCs and Level I non-corporate entities. Under the Companies Act 2013 all companies must prepare a CFS EXCEPT One Person Company, small company, dormant company, and (per 13 June 2017 notification) a start-up private company.
  • Transition: an enterprise not covered in the previous year but qualifying now need not show prior-year figures; one that qualified before but is now disqualified continues to prepare a CFS for two more consecutive years.
  • Objectives: assess ability to generate cash & cash equivalents, timing/certainty of future cash flows, liquidity/solvency, compare operational efficiency, and examine the relationship between profitability and net cash flow.

Key Definitions / Terms

Cash and Cash Equivalents
(a) Cash in hand and demand deposits with banks/financial institutions; (b) cash equivalents — short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible into known amounts of cash with insignificant risk of value change (maturity within 3 months of acquisition). Shares are not normally cash equivalents. At least three balance-sheet items; AS 3 requires a break-up of opening/closing cash as a note.
Cash Flows
Inflows (receipts) and outflows (payments) of cash and cash equivalents. Movements WITHIN cash/cash equivalents (e.g. cash deposited into bank, buying a cash-equivalent investment) are not cash flows.
Operating Activities
Principal revenue-producing activities and others that are not investing or financing (e.g. cash sales/purchases, collections from customers, payments to suppliers, salaries/wages).
Investing Activities
Acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents (e.g. purchase/sale of fixed assets, loans given/recovered by non-financial firms, interest/dividends earned by non-financial firms).
Financing Activities
Activities changing the size and composition of owners' capital (incl. preference capital) and borrowings (e.g. issue/redemption of shares/debentures, dividends paid, interest paid by non-financial firms).

How It Works / Logic

  • Classification depends on the nature of the enterprise. For financial enterprises, loans given, interest earned, investments and dividends in the ordinary course are OPERATING; for non-financial enterprises they are INVESTING (loans given) / the same. Loans to/from subsidiaries and interest thereon are investing (given) or financing (taken) for all; loans to employees and advances to suppliers are operating for all.
  • Dividends PAID are financing outflows for all enterprises. Income tax paid on operating income is operating; TDS follows the nature of the related income/expense (operating/investing/financing).
  • Insurance claims: against loss of stock/profits = extraordinary operating inflows; against loss of fixed assets = extraordinary investing inflows. Extraordinary cash flows are disclosed separately, classified as operating/investing/financing.
  • Direct method (operating): gross receipts (sales, trade receivables, services) less gross payments (purchases, creditors, operating expenses, wages, income tax), ignoring non-cash items like depreciation/provisions. Preferred, as it shows sources/uses clearly and aids forecasting.
  • Indirect method (operating): start from net profit/loss; add back non-cash, investing and financing items (depreciation, provisions, interest paid, loss on sale of assets) and deduct interest/dividend received; adjust for working-capital changes (excluding cash & cash equivalents) to reach net operating cash flow.
  • Profit/loss on sale of fixed assets is NOT operating — the entire proceeds go under investing.
  • Net-basis reporting: netting of investing/financing receipts and payments is forbidden (e.g. ₹50,000 machinery bought and ₹10,000 furniture sold are shown gross, not net ₹40,000). Exceptions — may report net: (a) receipts/payments on behalf of customers; (b) items with quick turnover, large amounts, short maturities. Financial enterprises may also net: fixed deposits with fixed maturity; placements/withdrawals with other financial enterprises; and advances/loans to customers and their repayments.
  • Interest & dividends: each disclosed separately. Financial enterprise — interest paid and interest/dividends received are operating. Other enterprises — interest paid is financing; interest/dividends received are investing; dividends paid are financing.
  • Non-cash transactions (e.g. bonus share issue, plant acquired by issue of debentures) are excluded from the CFS but disclosed elsewhere.
  • Business purchase (acquisition/disposal of subsidiaries/units): shown separately under investing, not netted; disclose total consideration and the portion discharged by cash. Current assets/liabilities taken over are excluded from operating working-capital changes.
  • Exchange gains/losses on foreign-currency cash balances are recognised in P&L and restated at balance-sheet-date rates, but are NOT cash flows; the resulting difference is reconciled in the CFS note. Foreign currency cash flows are recorded at the rate on the date of the cash flow.
  • Disclosures: significant cash/cash-equivalent balances held but not available for use (with management commentary); encouraged — undrawn borrowing facilities, and cash flows for maintaining vs increasing operating capacity.

Why It Matters / Application

Used with other statements, the CFS lets users evaluate changes in net assets, financial structure (liquidity and solvency), and the ability to affect the amount and timing of cash flows. Correct classification (operating/investing/financing) and the prohibition on netting are frequently examined.

Note

A change in cash does not always imply a cash flow (e.g. exchange restatement of a foreign bank balance). Gain on sale of a fixed asset is deducted (not added) under the indirect method, and the full sale proceeds appear in investing. Fixed deposits withdrawn by a bank's customers are operating cash flows for the bank.

Chapter 4 Unit 3 — AS 17: Segment Reporting

Source: 74690bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u3.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 17 establishes principles for reporting financial information about the different products/services an enterprise produces and the different geographical areas in which it operates, to help users understand performance, assess risks and returns, and judge the enterprise as a whole. Mandatory for Non-SMCs and Level I non-corporate entities.
  • Applied in general-purpose financial statements; comply fully, not selectively. Where a report has both consolidated and separate parent statements, segment information is given only on the consolidated basis.
  • Process: identify segments (business or geographical) → identify reportable segments → prepare segmental report and make disclosures.

Key Definitions / Terms

Business Segment
A distinguishable component providing an individual product/service or group of related products/services subject to risks and returns different from other segments. Factors: nature of products/services, nature of production processes, type/class of customers, distribution methods, and (if applicable) nature of the regulatory environment (banking, insurance, utilities).
Geographical Segment
A distinguishable component providing products/services within a particular economic environment subject to different risks/returns. Factors: similarity of economic/political conditions, relationships between operations, proximity, special area risks, exchange control, currency risks. May be based on location of assets/operations OR location of customers. May be a single country, a group of countries, or a region within a country.
Reportable Segment
A business or geographical segment for which AS 17 requires disclosure of segment information.
Segment Revenue
Directly attributable + reasonably allocable enterprise revenue + inter-segment transaction revenue. Excludes extraordinary items (AS 5), interest/dividend income, and gains on sale of investments/extinguishment of debt (unless the segment is primarily financial).
Segment Expense
Directly attributable + reasonably allocable expense + inter-segment transaction expense. Excludes extraordinary items, interest expense (unless financial segment or included in inventory cost per AS 16/AS 2), losses on investments/debt, income tax, and general/head-office enterprise-level expenses.
Segment Result / Assets / Liabilities
Result = segment revenue − segment expense. Segment assets/liabilities = operating assets/liabilities directly attributable or reasonably allocable; assets exclude income-tax assets and head-office assets; liabilities exclude income-tax liabilities and financing borrowings. Allocated jointly only if related revenues/expenses are also allocated.

How It Works / Logic

  • Interest treatment: interest expense on overdrafts/operating liabilities of a segment is excluded from segment expense unless the segment is primarily financial OR interest is included in inventory cost (AS 16 with AS 2); if included, disclose the amount and fact by note.
  • Allocation: the internal financial reporting system is the starting point; items identified with segments internally are presumed directly attributable/allocable. Determined before eliminating intra-enterprise balances/transactions (except within a single segment). Segment accounting policies include segment identification, inter-segment pricing method, and allocation bases, in addition to enterprise policies.
  • Primary vs secondary format: the dominant source and nature of risks and returns governs the primary format. If risks/returns are driven predominantly by products/services → primary = business segments, secondary = geographical; if driven by operating in different countries/areas → primary = geographical, secondary = business.
  • Business & geographical segments are normally based on internal reporting to the Board/CEO; if an internal segment does not meet the definition, look to the next lower level of internal segmentation along product/service or geographical lines.
  • Step I — 10% (Materiality) Test: a segment is reportable if ANY one holds — (a) Revenue (external + internal) ≥ 10% of total revenue (external + internal) of all segments; OR (b) Segment result (profit or loss) ≥ 10% of the greater, in absolute amount, of the combined result of all profit-making segments OR all loss-making segments; OR (c) Segment assets ≥ 10% of total assets of all segments.
  • Management may designate a segment reportable at its discretion despite failing the 10% test; if not so designated it is included as an unallocated reconciling item.
  • Step II — 75% (Overall) Test: if total EXTERNAL revenue of reportable segments is less than 75% of total enterprise revenue, add more segments (even if they fail the 10% tests) until at least 75% is included.
  • Continuity: a segment reportable in the preceding period stays reportable this period even if it now fails all 10% tests. A newly reportable segment's prior-period comparatives should be restated (unless impracticable).

Why It Matters / Application

Primary-format disclosures per reportable segment: segment revenue (split external and inter-segment), result, total segment assets, total segment liabilities, cost incurred to acquire fixed assets (tangible/intangible), depreciation/amortisation, and significant non-cash expenses. A reconciliation of segment revenue, result, assets and liabilities to the enterprise totals is required. Secondary-format disclosures apply the 10% threshold by geography or by business as appropriate. Also disclose inter-segment pricing basis and any change, changes in segment accounting policies (nature and financial effect), and the composition of each reported segment.

Note

The 10% revenue test uses BOTH internal and external revenue; the 75% test uses ONLY external revenue. The profit/loss test compares against the greater (in absolute figures) of total profits or total losses, not the net. Segment assets/liabilities exclude income-tax items. An enterprise disclosing segment cash flows need not also disclose depreciation/amortisation and non-cash expenses.

Chapter 4 Unit 4 — AS 18: Related Party Disclosures

Source: 74691bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u4.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 18 prescribes disclosure of related party relationships and transactions between the reporting enterprise and its related parties. It applies to each reporting enterprise's financial statements and to consolidated financial statements of a holding company.
  • Why needed: financial statements presume arm's-length dealings, but related parties may transact on non-market terms (or at no price), and the mere existence of a relationship may affect results even without a transaction — so the accounting measures may not represent what they otherwise would.

Key Definitions / Terms

Related Party
Parties are related if, at any time during the reporting period, one party can control the other or exercise significant influence over the other in making financial and/or operating decisions.
Related Party Transaction
A transfer of resources or obligations between related parties, regardless of whether or not a price is charged.
Control
(a) Ownership, directly/indirectly, of more than one-half of the voting power; OR (b) control of the composition of the board of directors (or governing body); OR (c) a substantial interest in voting power plus power to direct financial/operating policies by statute or agreement. Wider than AS 21 (clause (c) is additional). Substantial interest = 20% or more of voting power.
Significant Influence
Participation in financial/operating policy decisions but not control. Presumed at ≥20% voting power (rebuttable); presumed absent below 20% (rebuttable). May be gained by share ownership, statute or agreement; ways include board representation, policy participation, material inter-company transactions, interchange of managerial personnel, dependence on technical information.
Key Management Personnel (KMP)
Persons with authority and responsibility for planning, directing and controlling the activities of the reporting enterprise (e.g. MD, whole-time directors, manager, and persons whose directions the board is accustomed to act on). A non-executive director is not KMP merely by being a director.
Relative
Spouse, son, daughter, brother, sister, father and mother who may be expected to influence, or be influenced by, the individual in dealings with the reporting enterprise.
Associate / Joint Venture / Joint Control / Holding / Subsidiary / Fellow Subsidiary
Associate — enterprise in which the reporting party has significant influence, neither subsidiary nor JV. JV — contractual arrangement where two or more parties undertake an economic activity subject to joint control. Joint control — contractually agreed sharing of power over financial/operating policies. Holding company — one with one or more subsidiaries. Subsidiary — company where the holding controls >½ equity share capital or the composition of the board. Fellow subsidiaries — subsidiaries of the same holding company.

How It Works / Logic

  • Five types of related party relationships covered (definitive list): (a) enterprises that control, are controlled by, or are under common control with the reporting enterprise (holding, subsidiaries, fellow subsidiaries); (b) associates and joint ventures, and the investor/venturer; (c) individuals with voting-power interest giving control/significant influence, and their relatives; (d) KMP and their relatives; (e) enterprises over which any person in (c) or (d) exercises significant influence.
  • NOT related parties: (a) two companies merely sharing a common director (unless he can affect both companies' mutual dealings); (b) a single customer/supplier/franchiser/distributor/agent by mere economic dependence; (c) in the course of normal dealings — providers of finance, trade unions, public utilities, and government departments/agencies.
  • Exemptions from disclosure: (1) where disclosure conflicts with duties of confidentiality required by statute/regulator (e.g. banks re customers) — not for commercial-contract confidentiality; (2) intra-group transactions in consolidated financial statements; (3) state-controlled enterprises re relationships/transactions with other state-controlled enterprises.
  • Control of board composition = power, without others' consent, to appoint/remove all or a majority of directors. An enterprise is deemed to have power to appoint a director if the appointment needs its favour, follows from a position held, or is by its nomination.

Examples

  • A holds 51% B; B holds 51% O; Z holds 49% O. For O: A (ultimate holding), B (holding), Z (investor, O being Z's associate) are related. Associate of an associate is NOT related (X→Y 28%, Y→Z 32%: Z is not related to X).
  • A has 60% in B and 22% in C; B has 30% in C → A (with B) controls >½ of C, so C is related to A.
  • Mr Robert holds 70% of P Ltd; his spouse Andy is a related party (relative of a controlling individual). If Andy (spouse of MD) received remuneration during the year and left mid-year, she is still a related party at year-end because the relationship existed during the reporting period.
  • UK Bank holds 23% voting rights + nominee director in P Ltd: the provider-of-finance exemption does NOT apply because it exercises significant influence — a related party.
  • Narmada Ltd sold ₹90L to Ganga Ltd (MD owns 100% of Ganga) at normal prices — still a related party transaction requiring disclosure, regardless of price.
  • Where a relationship ceases mid-year, only transactions during its existence are disclosed (e.g. sale up to 30 June only, not later ordinary-customer sales).

Why It Matters / Application

Disclosure requirements: where control exists, disclose the name of the related party and the nature of the relationship — irrespective of whether transactions occurred. Where transactions occurred during the relationship, disclose (i) name of the transacting related party; (ii) description of the relationship; (iii) nature of transactions; (iv) volume (amount or proportion); (v) any other necessary elements; (vi) outstanding amounts at the balance sheet date and provisions for doubtful debts; (vii) amounts written off/back for such debts. Similar items may be aggregated by type unless separate disclosure is needed for understanding. Remuneration to KMP is a related party transaction; remuneration to non-executive directors (if not related parties) is not.

Note

Examples of transactions to disclose: purchases/sales of goods; purchases/sales of fixed assets; rendering/receiving services; agency arrangements; leasing/hire-purchase; transfer of R&D; licence agreements; finance (loans, equity contributions); guarantees and collaterals; management contracts including deputation of employees. Related parties are identified at ALL points during the year (not only at year-end), but transactions are disclosed only for the period the relationship existed.

Chapter 4 Unit 5 — AS 20: Earnings Per Share

Source: 74692bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u5.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 20 prescribes principles for the determination (computation) and presentation (on the face of the Statement of Profit and Loss) of earnings per share, improving comparison across enterprises for a period and across periods for one enterprise (inter-firm and intra-firm).
  • Mandatory for all companies. Disclosure of DILUTED EPS (including and excluding extraordinary items) is not mandatory for SMCs (encouraged). In consolidated financial statements, EPS is presented on the consolidated basis.
  • EPS is a financial ratio showing profit/loss for the period attributable to each equity share; the standard covers both basic and diluted EPS.

Key Definitions / Terms

Equity share / Preference share
Equity share = a share other than a preference share. Preference share = share carrying preferential rights to dividends and repayment of capital.
Potential Equity Share
A financial instrument or contract that entitles, or may entitle, its holder to equity shares — e.g. convertible debentures/preference shares, share warrants, options (incl. ESOPs), and contingently issuable shares. A partly paid-up share where the holder is not entitled to dividends is treated as a potential equity share for diluted EPS.
Financial instrument / asset / liability
Instrument — a contract giving rise to a financial asset of one enterprise and a financial liability/equity of another. Financial asset includes cash, contractual right to receive cash/another financial asset, favourable-exchange rights, or an equity share of another enterprise. Financial liability — contractual obligation to deliver cash/financial asset or exchange on unfavourable terms.
Fair value
Amount for which an asset could be exchanged, or a liability settled, between knowledgeable, willing parties in an arm's length transaction.
Time-weighting factor
(No. of days shares are outstanding) ÷ (No. of days in the period); a reasonable approximation (e.g. monthly) is acceptable.

How It Works / Logic

  • Basic EPS = Net profit/(loss) attributable to equity shareholders ÷ Weighted average number of equity shares outstanding during the period.
  • Numerator (basic): include all income and expense recognised in the period (tax, exceptional and extraordinary items, changes in estimates and policy per AS 5). Deduct preference dividends and attributable tax — for non-cumulative, only if provided; for cumulative, the full required amount whether or not provided (excluding arrears paid/declared this year for prior periods). If more than one class of equity shares, apportion profit by dividend rights (multiple basic EPS).
  • Denominator (basic): shares outstanding at the beginning, adjusted for shares issued/bought back, multiplied by the time-weighting factor. Shares are included from the date consideration is receivable (cash — when receivable; conversion of debt — date of conversion; in lieu of interest/principal — when interest ceases to accrue; settlement of a liability — settlement date; acquisition of a non-cash asset — recognition date; services rendered — as rendered).
  • Amalgamation: shares issued in a purchase are included from the date of acquisition; in a merger, from the beginning of the period. Partly paid shares are treated as a fraction to the extent entitled to participate in dividends. Contingently issuable shares are included in basic EPS from the date all conditions are met.
  • Bonus/split/reverse-split — no change in resources, so adjust retrospectively as if the event occurred at the beginning of the earliest period reported (restate prior EPS). Rights issue — usually contains a bonus element because the exercise price is below fair value; a rights issue at fair value has no bonus element.
  • Rights-issue adjustment factor = Fair value per share immediately prior to exercise ÷ Theoretical ex-rights fair value per share. Theoretical ex-rights value = (aggregate fair value of shares before exercise + proceeds from exercise) ÷ number of shares after exercise. Multiply pre-rights shares by this factor for all periods before the rights issue.
  • Diluted EPS: adjust the numerator by adding back (net of attributable tax) dividends and interest recognised on dilutive potential equity shares and any other changes in income/expense from conversion; increase the denominator by the weighted average additional shares assumed on conversion of all DILUTIVE potential equity shares (deemed converted at the beginning of the period, or the issue date if later, at the most advantageous rate for the holder).
  • Anti-dilutive potential equity shares (whose conversion would increase EPS or reduce loss per share from continuing ordinary activities) are IGNORED. The control figure is profit/loss from continuing ordinary activities. With multiple potential equity shares, each series is tested separately, in sequence from most to least dilutive (by earnings per incremental share).
  • Options: assume exercise; proceeds treated as issue of shares at fair value; the difference between shares issuable and shares that would be issued at fair value is treated as an issue for no consideration (dilutive). Fairly-priced shares are ignored. Share application/advance money utilised in the business is treated like dilutive potential equity shares.
  • Restatement: adjust basic and diluted EPS of all periods presented for bonus/split/reverse-split; if such changes occur after the balance sheet date but before approval, base per-share figures on the new number and disclose that fact.

Examples

Worked example 1 — Weighted average number of shares (Illustration 1). Shares outstanding move during the year: 1,800 for the first 5 months, then 2,400 (after a fresh issue) for the next 5 months, then 2,100 (after a buy-back) for the last 2 months. Weighting each block by its time-outstanding fraction:

  1. 1,800 × 5/12 = 750
  2. 2,400 × 5/12 = 1,000
  3. 2,100 × 2/12 = 350

Weighted average = 750 + 1,000 + 350 = 2,100 shares. Basic EPS would then be net profit attributable to equity shareholders ÷ 2,100.

Worked example 2 — Bonus issue restatement (Illustration 3). A 2-for-1 bonus (two new shares for each existing share) is made on 1 October 20X2, so shares rise from 20,00,000 to 60,00,000. Because a bonus issue brings in no fresh resources, it is treated as if it had always existed — the new count is used for the whole current year and retrospectively for the prior year:

  1. EPS 20X2 = ₹60,00,000 ÷ 60,00,000 shares = ₹1.00.
  2. EPS 20X1 as restated = ₹18,00,000 ÷ 60,00,000 shares = ₹0.30 (originally ₹18,00,000 ÷ 20,00,000 = ₹0.90, restated so the two years stay comparable).

Worked example 3 — Rights issue with a bonus element (Illustration 4). Net profit 20X1 ₹11,00,000; 20X2 ₹15,00,000; shares before the rights issue 5,00,000; rights ratio 1-for-5 (→ 1,00,000 new shares) at ₹15.00; last date to exercise 1 March 20X2; fair value immediately before exercise ₹21.00. A rights price below fair value contains a bonus element, so an adjustment factor is derived from the theoretical ex-rights price (TERP).

  1. Theoretical ex-rights price = (aggregate fair value before exercise + proceeds from exercise) ÷ shares after exercise = [(₹21.00 × 5,00,000) + (₹15.00 × 1,00,000)] ÷ (5,00,000 + 1,00,000) = (1,05,00,000 + 15,00,000) ÷ 6,00,000 = ₹1,20,00,000 ÷ 6,00,000 = ₹20.00.
  2. Adjustment factor = fair value per share prior to exercise ÷ TERP = ₹21.00 ÷ ₹20.00 = 1.05.
  3. EPS 20X1 as originally reported = ₹11,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 = ₹2.20; restated for the rights issue = ₹11,00,000 ÷ (5,00,000 × 1.05) = ₹11,00,000 ÷ 5,25,000 = ₹2.10.
  4. Weighted average shares for 20X2 = (5,00,000 × 1.05 × 2/12) + (6,00,000 × 10/12) = 87,500 + 5,00,000 = 5,87,500 shares (pre-rights shares are grossed up by the 1.05 factor for the part-year before the rights issue; the actual post-rights count applies afterwards).
  5. EPS 20X2 = ₹15,00,000 ÷ 5,87,500 = ₹2.55.

Worked example 4 — Diluted EPS with convertible debentures (Illustration 5). Net profit ₹1,00,00,000; 50,00,000 equity shares (basic EPS ₹2.00); 1,00,000 12% convertible debentures of ₹100 each, each convertible into 10 equity shares; tax rate 30%.

  1. Adjust the numerator by adding back post-tax interest saved on conversion: interest = 12% × (1,00,000 × ₹100) = ₹12,00,000; tax thereon at 30% = ₹3,60,000; net add-back = ₹8,40,000. Adjusted profit = 1,00,00,000 + 12,00,000 − 3,60,000 = ₹1,08,40,000.
  2. Adjust the denominator by the shares on assumed conversion: 1,00,000 × 10 = 10,00,000 shares → total = 50,00,000 + 10,00,000 = 60,00,000 shares.
  3. Diluted EPS = ₹1,08,40,000 ÷ 60,00,000 = ₹1.81. As ₹1.81 < basic ₹2.00, the debentures are dilutive and are included.

  • Options (Illustration 6): basic EPS ₹2.40; shares deemed issued for no consideration 25,000; diluted EPS = ₹12,00,000 ÷ 5,25,000 = ₹2.29.
  • Partly paid shares (Illustration 11): partly paid shares are included as a fraction to the extent entitled to dividends → weighted average 4,57,500 shares; basic EPS ₹4.80.

Why It Matters / Application

Presentation: present basic and diluted EPS on the face of the Statement of Profit and Loss for each class of equity share with a different right, with equal prominence for all periods — even if negative (loss per share). Disclosures: (a) where the P&L includes extraordinary items, basic and diluted EPS excluding extraordinary items (net of tax); (b) numerators used and reconciliation to net profit/loss; (c) weighted average denominators and their reconciliation to each other; (d) nominal value of shares along with EPS. Any additional per-share amounts must use the AS 20 weighted average and be disclosed with equal prominence.

Note

Bonus shares carry no consideration → treated as if issued at the earliest period's start (retrospective); shares at full market price carry no bonus element → time-weighted from issue date. All dilutive stock options (irrespective of vesting pattern) enter diluted EPS; lapsed options count only for the portion of the period outstanding.

Chapter 4 Unit 6 — AS 24: Discontinuing Operations

Source: 74693bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u6.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 24 establishes principles for reporting information about discontinuing operations, segregating them from continuing operations so users can better project cash flows, earnings-generating capacity and financial position. Applicable to ALL discontinuing operations.
  • It is essentially a presentation and disclosure standard — it does NOT provide recognition/measurement guidance; the relevant other Accounting Standards are referred to for that purpose.

Key Definitions / Terms

Discontinuing Operation
A component of an enterprise that: (a) pursuant to a single plan, is being (i) disposed of substantially in its entirety (single sale, demerger, spin-off), (ii) disposed of piecemeal (selling assets and settling liabilities individually), or (iii) terminated through abandonment; AND (b) represents a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations; AND (c) can be distinguished operationally and for financial reporting purposes.
Distinguished operationally & for financial reporting
Met if: (a) operating assets and liabilities can be directly attributed to it; (b) its revenue can be directly attributed to it; (c) at least a majority of its operating expenses can be directly attributed to it. Items are directly attributable if they would be eliminated on disposal (attributable debt carries its interest/financing costs).
Initial Disclosure Event
The EARLIER of: (a) the enterprise entering a binding sale agreement for substantially all the assets of the operation; or (b) the board/governing body BOTH approving a detailed, formal plan for discontinuance AND announcing it.

How It Works / Logic

  • A reportable business/geographical segment (AS 17) would normally qualify as a separate major line; a part of a segment may also qualify; for a single-segment enterprise not reporting segment information, a major product/service line may qualify.
  • Piecemeal disposal may span months and lacks a single binding-agreement date, but must be pursuant to a single coordinated plan. Abandonment without substantial asset sales can qualify; merely changing the scope/manner of an operation is NOT abandonment (the operation continues).
  • A detailed, formal plan normally includes: major assets to be disposed of; expected disposal method; period expected for completion; principal locations affected; location/function/approximate number of employees to be compensated; and estimated proceeds/salvage.
  • Announcement is made when the board communicates the plan's main features to those affected (lenders, stock exchanges, trade payables, trade unions, etc.) specifically enough to be demonstrably committed to discontinuance.
  • Discontinuing operations are infrequent, but not all infrequent events are discontinuing operations. Classification as a discontinuing operation does not by itself question the enterprise's going-concern ability.

Examples

  • Selling ONE restaurant (5% of revenue) out of a chain is not a discontinuing operation; selling an entire Airline business (25% of group revenue) via a single plan is.
  • Single-state trader RT selling Product Y (a major product line) — may be a discontinuing operation.
  • Activities NOT necessarily qualifying under criterion (a): gradual/evolutionary phasing out of a product line or class of service; abruptly discontinuing several products within an ongoing line; shifting production/marketing of a line from one location to another; closing a facility for productivity/cost savings.
  • Rohini Ltd: mere 20% annual gradual phase-out with no board resolution and no time-bound plan is NOT a discontinuing operation; a phased but time-bound board-resolved closure IS.

Why It Matters / Application

Initial disclosures (from the period of the initial disclosure event): (a) description of the operation; (b) segment(s) in which reported per AS 17; (c) date and nature of the initial disclosure event; (d) date/period discontinuance is expected to complete; (e) carrying amounts of total assets to be disposed of and liabilities to be settled; (f) revenue and expenses of its ordinary activities in the period; (g) pre-tax profit/loss from ordinary activities and related income-tax expense; (h) net operating, investing and financing cash flows in the period. On disposal/settlement or binding agreements: disclose the pre-tax gain/loss and related tax, and the net selling price (after disposal costs), expected timing of cash flows, and carrying amount of the net assets. Comparative prior periods are restated to segregate continuing and discontinuing operations.

Note

Most disclosures go in the notes, EXCEPT (shown on the face of the Statement of Profit and Loss): (a) pre-tax profit/loss from ordinary activities of the operation and its income-tax expense; and (b) pre-tax gain/loss on disposal of assets or settlement of liabilities. Disclosures are made separately for each discontinuing operation, updated for significant changes, and continue until discontinuance is completed (plan substantially completed or abandoned). If a plan is withdrawn, that fact, reasons and effect are disclosed. Interim reports disclose per AS 25.

Chapter 4 Unit 7 — AS 25: Interim Financial Reporting

Source: 74694bos60485-inter-p1-cp4-u7.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 25 does NOT mandate which enterprises must present interim financial reports, how frequently, or how soon after period-end. But if an enterprise is required or elects to prepare one, it must comply with AS 25.
  • It prescribes the minimum content of an interim financial report and the recognition/measurement principles for complete or condensed interim statements. Timely, reliable interim reporting improves users' understanding of earnings capacity, cash flows, financial condition and liquidity.
  • Where a statute/regulator requires interim information differing in form/content, AS 25's recognition and measurement principles still apply unless otherwise specified.

Key Definitions / Terms

Interim Period
A financial reporting period shorter than a full financial year.
Interim Financial Report
A financial report containing either a complete set of financial statements or a set of condensed financial statements for an interim period.
Complete set
Balance Sheet, Statement of Profit & Loss, Cash Flow Statement, and Notes (including accounting policies) — like an annual set.
Condensed set
At a minimum, each heading and sub-heading from the most recent annual statements plus selected explanatory notes; add line items/notes if omission would mislead. (First-year shorter reporting period is not an interim period.)

How It Works / Logic

  • Form and content: a complete interim set conforms to annual requirements; a condensed set includes the annual headings/sub-headings and selected notes. Basic and diluted EPS (per AS 20) are presented on the face of the interim P&L (complete or condensed) if presented annually.
  • Selected explanatory notes (if material and not disclosed elsewhere): (a) statement that the same accounting policies are followed (or nature/effect of changes); (b) seasonality of operations; (c) unusual items by nature/size/incidence (AS 5); (d) changes in estimates; (e) issuances/buy-backs/repayments/restructuring of debt/equity/potential equity; (f) dividends (aggregate or per share, by class); (g) segment revenue, segment result and segment capital employed (only if AS 17 requires segment disclosure annually); (h) effect of changes in composition (amalgamations, acquisition/disposal of subsidiaries, restructurings, discontinuing operations); (i) material changes in contingent liabilities. Normally reported year-to-date.
  • Periods presented: Balance Sheet — end of current interim period vs end of immediately preceding financial year. Statement of P&L — current interim period AND cumulative year-to-date vs comparable interim period and year-to-date of the preceding year. Cash Flow Statement — cumulative current year-to-date vs comparable year-to-date of the preceding year.
  • Materiality is assessed relative to the interim period data (interim measurements may rely more on estimates). Unusual/extraordinary items, changes in policy/estimates and prior-period items are recognised/disclosed by interim-period materiality.
  • Same accounting policies as annual statements (except policy changes to be reflected in the next annual statements). Reporting frequency must not affect annual results, so measurements are made on a year-to-date basis. Income-tax expense uses the best estimate of the weighted average annual effective tax rate; costs not meeting the asset definition are not deferred; losses (inventory write-downs, restructurings, impairments) follow annual principles, revised in later interim periods if estimates change.
  • Revenue received seasonally/occasionally (dividends, royalties, government grants; seasonal retailer revenue) is not anticipated or deferred unless that would be appropriate at year-end — recognised when it occurs. Costs incurred unevenly are anticipated/deferred only if appropriate to do so at year-end; deferral in the hope criteria are met before year-end is prohibited.
  • Use of estimates ensures reliable, relevant disclosure. Previously reported interim periods: a single policy is applied throughout the year; any policy change within the year is applied retrospectively to the beginning of the financial year (restatement).
  • Transitional provision: on first-time application, comparative interim P&L (current and year-to-date) and comparative year-to-date cash flow of the preceding year need not be presented. Disclosure in annual statements: if an estimate reported in an interim period changes significantly in the final interim period but no separate report is prepared for it, disclose the nature and amount in a note to the annual statements (AS 5).
  • Applicability: AS 25 presentation/disclosure requirements apply only to an 'interim financial report' as defined. Interim financial results not meeting the definition (e.g. quarterly results under Clause 41 of the Listing Agreement) need not follow the presentation/disclosure requirements, BUT the recognition and measurement principles still apply.

Examples

  • Sincere Corporation: expenditure uniform across quarters cannot be deferred to a high-sales quarter — Q1 profit ₹8 crores; company's view rejected.
  • X Ltd (Illustration 2): tax uses the year's average actual rate — Q1/Q2 at 20% (₹40 each), Q3/Q4 at 30% (₹60 each).
  • Poornima Ltd: 50% of bad debts (₹20,000) wrongly deferred is added back → correct quarterly income ₹7,00,000; extraordinary loss and additional depreciation correctly in the same quarter.
  • Weighted average annual tax rate (Illustration 10): estimated tax ₹3,50,000 ÷ ₹10,00,000 = 35%, applied to each quarter's income.
  • Asmitha Ltd: ₹2,00,000 disposal loss recognised fully in the interim period incurred; ₹60,000 property tax for the calendar year is accrued — ₹30,000 as expense for six months, ₹15,000 prior-year outstanding, ₹15,000 prepaid.
  • Warranty: Q1 provision 5% of ₹10m; fault found in Q2 raises rate to 10% for the year — Q2 charge ₹2 million (year-to-date basis).

Why It Matters / Application

Year-to-date measurement prevents interim reporting frequency from distorting annual results and stops arbitrary smoothing of earnings across quarters. Defined-benefit provisions (pension, gratuity) are computed year-to-date using actuarially determined rates from the prior year-end, adjusted for significant market fluctuations, curtailments, settlements or one-time events. Impairment losses recognised in one quarter can be reversed in a later quarter of the same year if a favourable indicator exists and recoverable amount rises (AS 28).

Note

A change in depreciation method is a change in accounting estimate (AS 10 with AS 5) applied prospectively — no restatement adjustment. Cumulative loss from a change in inventory valuation method is applied retrospectively. Seasonal/occasional income (dividend, extraordinary gain, gain on sale of investment) is recognised when it occurs, not spread across quarters.

Chapter 5 Unit 1 — AS 2: Valuation of Inventory

Source: 87702bos-aps2158-ch5u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 2 (Revised) ‘Valuation of Inventories’ determines the value at which inventories are carried until related revenues are recognised, the cost formulas used to assign costs, and any write-down to net realisable value (NRV).
  • Fundamental rule: Inventory = lower of cost and net realisable value. Prudence demands no profit be anticipated while all foreseeable losses are recognised — if NRV < cost, value at NRV; if NRV > cost, ignore the anticipated profit and value at cost.
  • Valuation directly affects profit: higher closing inventory → lower cost of goods sold → higher profit; hence prudence prevents overstatement.
  • Comparison of cost and NRV is made on an item-by-item basis (similar/related items may be grouped). Global/overall valuation is not appropriate.
  • Inventory costs are those that generate future economic benefits — costs of acquisition and costs of bringing inventory to present location (e.g. freight to factory) and condition (e.g. conversion). Maintenance/storage costs are excluded unless necessary in production before a further stage.
  • Abnormal gains/losses are not taken into inventory cost; they are shown in the P&L statement so their impact on current profit is visible.
  • Scope exclusions: (a) WIP under construction contracts (AS 7) — but inventory held for use in construction, e.g. cement at site, is covered by AS 2; (b) WIP of service providers (e.g. shipping voyage-in-progress, software, consultancy, medical, merchant banking); (c) shares/debentures/financial instruments held as stock-in-trade (also excluded from AS 13; valued at lower of cost and fair value); (d) producers’ inventories of livestock, agricultural/forest products, mineral oils/ores/gases measured at NRV per established industry practice.

Key Definitions / Terms

Inventories
Assets held (a) for sale in the ordinary course of business, (b) in the process of production for such sale, or (c) for consumption in production of goods/services, including maintenance supplies and consumables (but not spares/servicing/standby equipment meeting the PPE definition under AS 10).
Net Realisable Value (NRV)
Estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less estimated costs of completion and estimated costs necessary to make the sale.
Cost of purchase
Purchase price + duties and taxes (other than those recoverable from taxing authorities) + other directly attributable acquisition expenditure, less trade discounts, rebates, duty drawbacks and similar items.
Cost of conversion
Costs directly related to production (e.g. direct labour) + fixed and variable production overheads incurred in converting raw material into finished goods.
Normal capacity
Production the enterprise expects to achieve on average over a number of periods/seasons under normal circumstances, taking into account loss from planned maintenance (actual level used if it approximates normal).

How It Works / Logic

  • Cost of inventories = costs of purchase + costs of conversion + other costs to bring inventory to present location and condition.
  • Fixed production overheads absorbed systematically on normal capacity. Allocation per unit is not increased due to low production/idle plant; unallocated (under-recovered) overhead is expensed. In periods of abnormally high production, per-unit fixed overhead is decreased so inventory is not measured above cost. Variable overheads assigned on actual use of facilities.
  • Joint/by-products: costs up to split-off allocated on a rational, consistent basis (e.g. sale value at split-off). By-products, scrap and waste (usually immaterial) valued at NRV, which is deducted from the joint cost to value the main product.
  • Other costs included only if they bring inventory to present location/condition (e.g. design cost of a custom unit). Interest/borrowing costs normally excluded unless inventory takes a substantial period to be ready for sale (e.g. wine). Amortisation of intangibles related to production (e.g. patent/copyright for a publisher) may be included. Exchange differences excluded.
  • Exclusions from cost (expensed as incurred): (a) abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or production costs; (b) storage costs unless necessary in the production process prior to a further stage; (c) administrative overheads not contributing to location/condition; (d) selling and distribution costs.
  • Cost formulas: use specific identification where possible; otherwise FIFO or Weighted Average. The formula chosen should reflect the fairest approximation of cost incurred.
  • Other techniques: (a) Standard cost may be used if it approximates actual (set on normal levels of material, labour efficiency, capacity utilisation; reviewed/revised regularly); (b) Retail method for large numbers of rapidly changing items — cost = sale value of unsold stock less an appropriate average gross-margin percentage.
  • Materials held for use are not written down below cost if the finished product will sell at or above cost. If a decline means finished product cost will exceed NRV, materials are written down to NRV, and replacement cost may be the best available measure of that NRV.
  • NRV estimates use the most reliable evidence available at estimation date and consider post-balance-sheet price/cost fluctuations to the extent they confirm conditions existing at the balance sheet date. NRV is reassessed at each balance sheet date.

Examples

  • Lower of cost/NRV (Ex.1): Cost ₹150; NRV = ₹250 − ₹100 completion − ₹10 brokerage (4% of 250) = ₹140 → valued at ₹140.
  • Normal capacity (Ex.2): Fixed OH ₹18 lacs, normal capacity 1 lac units. At 90,000 units include ₹16.2 lacs (90,000 × ₹18), balance ₹1.8 lacs to P&L; at 1.2 lac units use actual ₹15/unit (₹18 lacs total) so no unincurred cost is added.
  • Retail method (Ex.3): Sale value of stock ₹20,000; gross markup 25% on cost → margin ₹4,000 (20,000/1.25 × 0.25) → cost of inventory ₹16,000.
  • Item-by-item (Illus.1): Products A,B,C cost ₹88L vs NRV ₹84L → closing stock valued item-wise at ₹76 lakhs.
  • Abnormal waste (Illus.3): Input 5,000 MT @ ₹1,000; normal waste 250 MT, abnormal 50 MT. Cost/MT on normal quantity 4,750 = ₹1,052.63; abnormal loss ₹52,632 to P&L; inventory 4,700 MT × ₹1,052.63 = ₹49,47,368.
  • Materials + FG (Illus. Mehul): Raw material written to replacement cost ₹90 (finished product sells below cost); partly finished at NRV ₹240 (300 − 60); finished at NRV ₹300 (< cost ₹320).

Chapter 5 Unit 2 — AS 10: Property, Plant & Equipment

Source: 87703bos-aps2158-ch5u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 10 (Revised) prescribes the accounting treatment for PPE so users understand information about an enterprise’s investment in PPE and changes in it. Principal issues: recognition, determination of carrying amounts, depreciation charges and impairment losses.
  • Scope: applied to all PPE except where another standard requires/permits different treatment (e.g. AS 19 Leases governs recognition of leased PPE via transfer of risks/rewards, but depreciation etc. follow AS 10).
  • Not applicable to: biological assets (other than bearer plants) related to agricultural activity; wasting assets/mineral rights and expenditure on exploration/extraction of minerals, oil, gas and similar non-regenerative resources. AS 10 applies to bearer plants but not to the produce on them. Intangibles are under AS 26. Investment property is accounted for only under the AS 10 cost model.
  • Recognition: cost of a PPE item is recognised as an asset only if (a) it is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the enterprise, and (b) the cost can be measured reliably. Individually insignificant items (moulds, tools, dies) may be aggregated; immaterial items may be expensed.
  • PPE acquired for safety/environmental reasons qualifies because it enables future economic benefits from other assets; such carrying amounts are reviewed for impairment under AS 28.

Key Definitions / Terms

PPE
Tangible items (Condition 1) held for use in production/supply of goods/services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes (used in a wide sense — includes selling/distribution, finance/accounting, personnel functions); and (Condition 2) expected to be used for more than 12 months.
Bearer plant
A plant used in producing/supplying agricultural produce, expected to bear produce for >12 months, with only a remote likelihood of being sold as agricultural produce (except incidental scrap sales, e.g. firewood). Annual crops, trees grown for lumber, and plants likely to be sold as produce are not bearer plants.
Depreciable amount
Cost (or the revalued amount substituted for cost) less residual value, allocated systematically over useful life.
Useful life
The period over which an asset is expected to be available for use, or the number of production/similar units expected from it.
Residual value
Estimated amount currently obtainable on disposal at the end of useful life (net of disposal costs), assuming the asset were already of that age/condition.
Class of PPE
A grouping of assets of similar nature and use (e.g. land; land & buildings; machinery; ships; aircraft; motor vehicles; furniture & fixtures; office equipment; bearer plants).

How It Works / Logic

  • Elements of cost: (A) purchase price incl. import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, less trade discounts/rebates; (B) directly attributable costs of bringing the asset to the location/condition for intended operation (employee benefits from construction/acquisition, site preparation, initial delivery & handling, installation/assembly, testing net of sale proceeds of samples, professional fees); (C) initial estimate of dismantling/removal/restoration (“decommissioning”) liabilities, recognised/measured per AS 29 (obligations arising from producing inventory follow AS 2).
  • Excluded from cost (expensed): costs of opening a new facility (inauguration), introducing a new product/service (advertising/promotion), conducting business in a new location/with a new class of customer (staff training), administration/general overheads, initial operating losses, costs while item is below full capacity, relocating/reorganising costs. Recognition ceases once the item is in the location/condition for intended operation.
  • Incidental income during development: directly attributable (e.g. sale of demolition debris) adjusts cost; not directly attributable (e.g. car-park rental on the site) recognised in P&L.
  • Self-constructed asset: same principles as acquired; eliminate internal profits; exclude abnormal wastage; capitalise borrowing costs per AS 16; bearer plants treated like self-constructed PPE until ready.
  • Exchange of assets (non-monetary / mixed): measured at fair value unless the transaction lacks commercial substance or fair value of neither asset is reliably measurable — then at carrying amount of the asset given up. Commercial substance depends on whether post-tax cash flows change significantly. Preference order: FV of asset given up → FV of asset received → WDV of asset given up.
  • Special cost cases: cost = cash-price equivalent; deferred payment beyond normal credit → excess is interest expense (unless capitalised per AS 16); consolidated price apportioned on relative fair values (competent valuers if FV not reliable); finance-lease PPE per AS 19; carrying amount may be reduced by government grants per AS 12.
  • Subsequent costs: day-to-day servicing (repairs & maintenance) expensed. Replacement of parts and regular major inspections capitalised if recognition criteria met, and the carrying amount of the old part/previous inspection is derecognised. Revised WDV = WDV of asset + cost of new part − WDV of old part (old-part WDV from supplier’s invoice breakup, or FV at purchase, or estimated from replacement cost, then less depreciation).
  • Measurement after recognition (chosen per entire class): Cost model = cost − accumulated depreciation − accumulated impairment; Revaluation model = fair value at revaluation date − subsequent accumulated depreciation − subsequent impairment. If one item is revalued, the whole class is revalued (avoids selective revaluation and mixed-date amounts).
  • Revaluation mechanics: Technique 1 — gross carrying amount and accumulated depreciation restated proportionately (or by market data); Technique 2 — accumulated depreciation eliminated against gross carrying amount. Fair value from market-based appraisal by qualified valuers, or (if specialised) income/depreciated-replacement-cost approaches. Frequency: annual for volatile fair values, every 3–5 years for insignificant changes.
  • Revaluation surplus: an increase is credited directly to owners’ interests as revaluation surplus (unless it reverses a prior decrease charged to P&L); a decrease is charged to P&L (unless reversing an existing surplus for that asset). Surplus may be transferred to revenue reserves on derecognition, or progressively as used (difference between depreciation on revalued vs original cost) — never through the P&L.
  • Depreciation: component method — each significant part depreciated separately (parts with same life/method may be grouped). Charge recognised in P&L unless included in another asset’s carrying amount (AS 2 conversion cost; AS 26 development). Begins when available for use; not affected by idleness (unless usage method or fully depreciated). Ceases at the earlier of held-for-disposal date and derecognition; and depreciation is zero if residual value ≥ carrying amount. Methods: straight-line (constant charge), diminishing balance (decreasing charge), units of production (charge on output). Revenue-based methods are not appropriate.
  • Land vs buildings: separable and accounted for separately even if acquired together. Land has unlimited life (not depreciated) except quarries/landfill sites, land with limited life, or land cost including dismantlement/restoration. Buildings have limited life and are depreciable; a rise in land value does not affect the building’s depreciable amount.
  • Review (each year-end, AS 5, prospective): residual value, useful life and depreciation method are reviewed at least annually; changes are treated as changes in accounting estimate.
  • Changes in decommissioning/restoration liabilities: under the cost model, add to/deduct from asset cost (deduction not exceeding carrying amount; excess to P&L; test for impairment if addition indicates non-recoverability). Under the revaluation model, adjust the revaluation surplus/deficit. Once the asset’s useful life ends, all subsequent liability changes go to P&L.
  • Compensation for impairment: impairment per AS 28; derecognition per AS 10; third-party compensation included in P&L when receivable (recognised under AS 29 when receipt virtually certain, measured at fair value of assets to be provided); replacement cost per AS 10.
  • Retirement & derecognition: items retired and held for disposal stated at lower of carrying amount and NRV (write-down to P&L). Derecognise on disposal (sale, finance lease, donation) or when no future benefits expected. Gain/loss = net disposal proceeds − carrying amount, to P&L (gains not classified as revenue per AS 9). Enterprises routinely selling ex-rental assets transfer them to inventory at carrying amount and recognise proceeds as revenue.

Chapter 5 Unit 3 — AS 13: Accounting for Investments

Source: 87704bos-aps2158-ch5u3.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 13 (Revised) deals with accounting for investments in financial statements and related disclosures. Shares/debentures/securities held as stock-in-trade are not ‘investments’, but current-investment provisions apply to them with suitable modifications.
  • Does not deal with: recognition of interest/dividends/rentals earned (AS 9); operating/finance leases; investments of retirement-benefit plans and life insurance enterprises; and mutual funds, venture capital funds/their AMCs, banks and public financial institutions under a Central/State Act.
  • Investments are classified as Current or Long-term; classification depends on intention judged at the time of purchase, not on marketability (even a readily marketable holding can be long-term).
  • Carrying amount: current investments at lower of cost and fair value (valued individually, not on an overall/global basis); long-term investments at cost, reduced only for a decline that is other than temporary.

Key Definitions / Terms

Investments
Assets held by an enterprise for earning income (dividends, interest, rentals), for capital appreciation, or for other benefits; stock-in-trade/inventory is not an investment.
Current investment
An investment that is by its nature readily realisable and intended to be held for not more than one year from the date made.
Long-term investment
An investment other than a current investment.
Fair value
The amount for which an asset could be exchanged between a knowledgeable, willing buyer and seller in an arm’s length transaction (market value or NRV may evidence it).
Market value
Amount obtainable from sale in an open market, net of expenses necessarily incurred on/before disposal.
Investment property
Investment in land/buildings not intended to be occupied substantially for use in the investing enterprise’s operations; accounted for under the AS 10 cost model.

How It Works / Logic

  • Forms/classification: investments may be physical (buildings) or documentary (shares). Beyond current/long-term, statutory further classification applies; absent statute, disclose: (a) Government/Trust securities, (b) shares/debentures/bonds, (c) investment properties, (d) others (specify).
  • Cost of investment = purchase price + acquisition charges (brokerage, fees, duties). If acquired by issue of shares/securities, cost = fair value of securities issued (not necessarily par). If acquired in exchange for another asset, cost = FV of asset given up or of investment acquired, whichever is more clearly evident.
  • Pre-acquisition income: interest accrued before acquisition and included in the price is deducted from cost when received (allocated pre/post acquisition); pre-acquisition dividends similarly reduce cost only if they clearly represent recovery of cost.
  • Right shares: if subscribed, cost added to carrying amount of the original holding; if sold, proceeds to P&L. Exception — where acquired cum-right and the ex-right market value falls below acquisition cost, rights sale proceeds may be applied to reduce carrying amount to market value.
  • Current investments: any reduction to fair value is debited to P&L; a subsequent increase is credited to P&L only up to cost (excess ignored).
  • Long-term investments: a decline other than temporary (e.g. investee’s cash operating losses reducing net worth, adverse new regulation, significant fall in quoted price) is charged to P&L for each investment individually; reversed when value rises or the reasons cease.
  • Investment properties: accounted for under the AS 10 cost model; cost of shares in a co-op/company whose holding gives the right to hold the property is added to the property’s carrying amount.
  • Disposal: difference between carrying amount and net disposal proceeds recognised in P&L. On part disposal, carrying amount allocated on the average carrying amount of the total holding (for stock-in-trade, an appropriate cost formula — FIFO/average — as in AS 2).
  • Reclassification: long-term → current at lower of cost and carrying amount at date of transfer; current → long-term at lower of cost and fair value at date of transfer.

Examples

  • Cost (Ex.): Long-term deposit ₹200L + brokerage ₹1L → carried at ₹201 lakhs.
  • Diminution (Illus.1): Long-term at cost ₹2 lakhs, likely to fetch only ₹20,000 → provide for the other-than-temporary decline, reduce carrying amount to ₹20,000.
  • Current vs long-term (Illus.2): ₹600L in Y Ltd, 50:50 long-term/current; realisable value falls to ₹200L (not temporary) → both halves reduced to ₹100L each, ₹200L each charged to P&L.
  • Reclassification (Illus.3): Current cost ₹20L, market ₹25L → to long-term at cost ₹20L; current cost ₹15L, market ₹6.5L → at ₹6.5L (loss ₹8.5L to P&L); long-term cost ₹18L written to ₹12L → to current at carrying ₹12L.
  • Investment account — bonus/rights: bonus shares carry nil cost; rights sold credited to P&L; closing balance valued at average cost (e.g. TELCO shares closing ₹82,000 vs market ₹90,000 → carried at lower ₹82,000).
  • Gold/silver (Illus.4): Held long-term, no other-than-temporary decline → retained at cost (₹4,00,000 & ₹2,00,000) despite higher market values; short-term shares at lower of cost & market.

Chapter 5 Unit 4 — AS 16: Borrowing Costs

Source: 87705bos-aps2158-ch5u4.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 16 prescribes the accounting treatment for borrowing costs. It does not deal with the actual or imputed cost of owners’ equity, including preference share capital not classified as a liability.
  • Coverage of funds: equity share capital — No; retained earnings — No; preference capital classified as a liability — Yes; preference capital classified as equity — No.
  • Borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset are capitalised as part of its cost (they are those that would have been avoided had the expenditure not been made); other borrowing costs are expensed in the period incurred.
  • Recognition criteria for capitalisation: (a) it is probable they will result in future economic benefits, and (b) the costs can be measured reliably.

Key Definitions / Terms

Borrowing costs
Interest and other costs incurred in connection with borrowing of funds — interest & commitment charges; amortisation of discount/premium on borrowings; amortisation of ancillary costs; finance charges on finance-lease assets; and exchange differences to the extent regarded as an adjustment to interest cost.
Qualifying asset
An asset (tangible or intangible) that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale (e.g. manufacturing plants, power facilities, investment properties, inventories needing substantial time). Assets ready when acquired, and routinely mass-produced inventories, are not qualifying assets.
Substantial period of time
Depends on facts/circumstances; a rebuttable presumption of twelve months unless a shorter/longer period is justified. Considers time technologically and commercially needed to get ready.
Capitalisation rate
The weighted average of borrowing costs on the enterprise’s borrowings outstanding during the period, other than borrowings made specifically for a qualifying asset.

How It Works / Logic

  • Specific borrowings: amount eligible for capitalisation = actual borrowing costs incurred on that borrowing during the period less any income on the temporary investment of those borrowings (funds are often invested temporarily before being spent on the asset).
  • General borrowings: (Step 1) capitalisation rate = borrowing cost on general borrowings ÷ weighted average general borrowings outstanding × 100; (Step 2) eligible amount = expenditure on the qualifying asset × capitalisation rate; (Step 3) cross-check — amount capitalised must not exceed borrowing costs incurred in the period. The average carrying amount of the asset (incl. previously capitalised costs) approximates the expenditure to which the rate is applied.
  • Foreign-currency borrowings (para 4(e)): exchange differences on principal are treated as borrowing cost only to the extent they do not exceed the difference between interest on local-currency and foreign-currency borrowings (local rate = the rate at which the enterprise would have borrowed locally). Exchange loss — lower of actual loss and the interest differential is borrowing cost; excess to P&L (AS 11). Exchange gain credited to P&L; a later gain reduces that year’s borrowing cost to the extent an exchange loss was earlier treated as borrowing cost.
  • Commencement when all conditions hold: (a) expenditure on the asset is being incurred (cash/other assets/interest-bearing liabilities, reduced by progress payments and grants); (b) borrowing costs are being incurred; and (c) activities necessary to prepare the asset for intended use/sale are in progress (includes technical/administrative work; excludes merely holding an undeveloped asset — e.g. land held without development does not qualify).
  • Suspension during extended periods in which active development is interrupted (costs of holding partially completed assets). Not suspended during substantial technical/administrative work, or during a necessary temporary delay (e.g. inventory maturing; high water levels common in the region delaying a bridge).
  • Cessation when substantially all activities to prepare the asset for intended use/sale are complete (routine admin/minor decoration outstanding still means complete). For an asset completed in parts, cessation applies part-by-part where a completed part can be used (e.g. business park); an asset needing all parts before use (e.g. sequential steel mill) is treated as a whole.
  • Excess over recoverable amount: where carrying amount/expected ultimate cost exceeds recoverable amount or NRV, the asset is written down/off per other standards (with write-back where those standards allow).

  • Proportionate (Illus.1): Loan ₹120L; only shed ₹50L is a qualifying asset → capitalise 18 × 50/120 = ₹7.5L; ₹10.5L to P&L (machinery, working capital, truck advance not qualifying).
  • Mixed borrowings (Illus.2): Specific ₹1L @ 10% = ₹10,000 + general (₹5,22,500 × 12.285%) = ₹64,189 → ₹74,189 capitalised; building capitalised at ₹10,94,189.
  • FCY loan (Ex.): Principal increase ₹30,000; interest differential ₹25,500 → borrowing cost ₹25,500 (total ₹49,500 under AS 16), ₹4,500 under AS 11. If local rate 13%, entire ₹30,000 is borrowing cost.
  • Rainbow Ltd (Q10): Specific-borrowing interest ₹16.5cr (11% of 150cr) − ₹3.5cr temporary income → capitalise ₹13 crores; using WACC 13% is incorrect.
  • Phases (Q11): Interest ₹30L on ₹200L loan; Phases I&II (operational mid-year): half of ₹13,30,317 capitalised (34:64), half expensed; Phases III&IV interest ₹16,69,683 held in CWIP.
  • Expert Ltd (Q12): Eligible cost = ₹10L (100L × 12% × 10/12) − ₹50,000 = ₹9,50,000; only factory building qualifies → capitalise ₹3,80,000, rest ₹5,70,000 to P&L.

Chapter 5 Unit 5 — AS 19: Leases

Source: 87706bos-aps2158-ch5u5.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • A lease is an agreement whereby the lessor (legal owner of an asset) conveys to the lessee, in return for a payment or series of periodic payments (lease rents), the right to use an asset for an agreed period of time.
  • Objective of AS 19: prescribe, for lessees and lessors, appropriate accounting policies and disclosures for finance leases and operating leases.
  • Scope (para 5.2): applies to all leases except (a) lease agreements to explore for or use natural resources (oil, gas, timber, metals, mineral rights); (b) licensing agreements for motion picture films, video recordings, plays, manuscripts, patents and copyrights; and (c) lease agreements to use land.
  • Classification depends on the substance of the transaction rather than its form: a lease is a finance lease if it transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incident to ownership (title may or may not eventually transfer); otherwise it is an operating lease. Basis of classification is the risk-and-reward test.
  • Special issues covered: what a lease is and how to classify it (from both lessee's and lessor's viewpoint); accounting in the books of lessee and lessor; and sale-and-leaseback transactions.
  • Hire purchase agreements (property passes to hirer on payment of the last instalment, with a right to terminate before) are included within the definition of a lease.

Key Definitions / Terms

Non-cancellable lease
A lease cancellable only: on a remote contingency; with the lessor's permission; if the lessee enters a new lease for the same/equivalent asset with the same lessor; or on payment of an additional amount such that continuation is reasonably certain at inception.
Lease term
The non-cancellable period plus any further periods the lessee has the option to continue (with or without further payment) which, at inception, it is reasonably certain the lessee will exercise.
Inception of the lease
The earlier of the date of the lease agreement and the date of a commitment by the parties to the principal provisions of the lease.
Minimum Lease Payments (MLP)
Payments over the lease term the lessee is (or can be) required to make, excluding contingent rent, service costs and taxes reimbursed to the lessor, plus: for the lessee — residual value guaranteed by/on behalf of the lessee; for the lessor — residual value guaranteed by/on behalf of the lessee or by an independent, financially capable third party. If a bargain purchase option exists, MLP also includes the payment to exercise that option.
Fair value
Amount for which an asset could be exchanged, or a liability settled, between knowledgeable, willing parties in an arm's length transaction.
Economic life
The period over which an asset is expected to be economically usable by one or more users, or the number of production units expected. Economic life is always greater than useful life.
Useful life of a leased asset
The period over which the leased asset is expected to be used by the lessee (or units expected from it); represents the depreciable life.
Residual value / GRV / UGRV
Residual value = estimated fair value at the end of the lease term = Guaranteed Residual Value (GRV) + Unguaranteed Residual Value (UGRV). GRV is the part guaranteed by/on behalf of the lessee (or independent third party); UGRV is the excess of residual value over GRV.
Gross Investment (GI)
Undiscounted total cash inflows to the lessor = MLP + UGRV.
Unearned Finance Income (UFI)
GI − (PV of MLP + PV of UGRV) at the implicit rate = GI − Net Investment = GI − Fair Value; simply, the total interest.
Net Investment (NI)
GI − UFI = discounted (present) value of GI = Fair Value.
Interest rate implicit in the lease
Discount rate at which, at inception, (PV of MLP + PV of UGRV) = fair value of the leased asset; i.e. cash outflow (fair value) = PV of cash inflows (lease payments + residual value). Effectively the lessor's IRR.
Lessee's incremental borrowing rate
The rate the lessee would pay on a similar lease, or (if not determinable) the rate to borrow, over a similar term with similar security, the funds to purchase the asset.
Contingent rent
Portion of lease payments not fixed in amount but based on a factor other than passage of time (e.g. percentage of sales, usage, price indices, market interest rates).

How It Works / Logic

  • Indicators of a finance lease — 8 parameters: 5 deterministic (any one met ⇒ finance lease) and 3 suggestive (even all met need not imply a finance lease). Classification is made at inception.
  • Deterministic conditions (para 5.6): (a) ownership transfers to the lessee by end of term; (b) bargain purchase option — price expected to be sufficiently below fair value that exercise is reasonably certain; (c) lease term is for the major part of the economic life even if title is not transferred; (d) at inception, PV of MLP ≥ substantially all of the fair value; (e) asset is of specialised nature usable only by the lessee without major modification.
  • Suggestive conditions (para 5.7): (a) lessee bears the lessor's cancellation losses; (b) gains/losses from fluctuations in residual value accrue to the lessee; (c) lessee can continue for a secondary period at rent substantially below market.
  • A change to lease provisions (other than renewal) that would have altered classification is treated as a new agreement. Changes in estimates (economic life, residual value) or circumstances (lessee default) do not give rise to reclassification.
  • Finance lease — books of lessee: at inception recognise an asset and a corresponding liability at the lower of fair value and PV of MLP (discount at implicit rate if practicable, else lessee's incremental borrowing rate). The liability is shown separately (current/long-term), not as a deduction from the asset. Apportion each lease payment between finance charge and reduction of liability, allocating finance charges to produce a constant periodic rate of interest on the remaining liability. Charge depreciation per AS 10 consistent with owned assets. Initial direct costs attributable to the lease are added to the asset.
  • Depreciation period (lessee): if ownership is reasonably certain to be obtained ⇒ useful life; if not certain ⇒ shorter of useful life and lease term.
  • Finance lease — books of lessor: recognise the asset as a receivable at an amount equal to net investment in the lease (= fair value). Profit at inception = Fair Value − Cost; total earning = GI − Cost = UFI + (Fair Value − Cost). Recognise finance income over the lease term on a basis reflecting a constant periodic return on the net investment outstanding. Initial direct costs (commissions, legal fees) are recognised immediately or allocated against finance income over the term.
  • Review of UGRV (lessor): review regularly; a reduction in estimated UGRV requires revising income allocation over the remaining term and recognising immediately any reduction of already-accrued income. Upward adjustment of residual value is not made.
  • Manufacturer/dealer lessor: recognise sale profit per the enterprise's outright-sale policy; if interest quoted is artificially low, restrict profit to a commercial-rate basis. Initial direct costs are expensed at inception.
  • Operating lease — lessee: recognise lease payments as an expense on a straight-line basis over the term unless another systematic basis is more representative of benefit; differences between rent due and rent recognised go to Lease Equalisation A/c (closes in the terminal year).
  • Operating lease — lessor: present the asset as PPE; recognise lease income on a straight-line basis; charge depreciation per its normal policy (AS 10) and impairment per AS 28. Initial direct costs are deferred and allocated to income, or expensed as incurred. A manufacturer/dealer lessor recognises no selling profit on entering an operating lease.

Chapter 5 Unit 6 — AS 26: Intangible Assets

Source: 87707bos-aps2158-ch5u6.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Objective: prescribe the accounting treatment for intangible assets not dealt with specifically in another standard; requires recognition only if defined criteria are met, specifies measurement of carrying amount and disclosures.
  • An intangible asset is an identifiable, non-monetary asset, without physical substance, held for use in the production/supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes.
  • Three essential ingredients: identifiability, control over a resource, and expectation (probable — 50%+) of future economic benefits. If an item within AS 26's scope fails the definition, expenditure to acquire/generate it is expensed when incurred.
  • Scope excludes: intangibles covered by other standards — held-for-sale in the ordinary course (AS 2, AS 7), deferred tax assets (AS 22), leases within AS 19, goodwill on amalgamation (AS 14) and on consolidation (AS 21); financial assets; mineral/oil/gas rights and exploration/extraction expenditure; insurance-contract intangibles; and termination benefits.
  • Applies to: other intangibles (e.g. computer software) and expenditure (e.g. start-up costs) in extractive/insurance industries; advertising, training, start-up costs; R&D activities; rights under licensing agreements (films, video, plays, manuscripts, patents, copyrights — excluded from AS 19); and the underlying intangible in a finance lease after initial recognition.
  • Where an asset combines intangible and tangible elements, judgement decides the predominant element (e.g. operating system / machine-control software integral to hardware ⇒ treated as a fixed asset).

Key Definitions / Terms

Amortisation
Systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an intangible asset over its useful life.
Depreciable amount
Cost of an asset less its residual value.
Useful life
The period over which an asset is expected to be used, or the number of production units expected from it.
Monetary / non-monetary assets
Monetary = money held and assets to be received in fixed/determinable amounts of money; non-monetary = all others.
Identifiability
The asset is clearly distinguishable from goodwill — separable (can be rented, sold, exchanged or distributed), or arising from legal/contractual rights. Separability is not essential if the asset can otherwise be identified.
Control
Power to obtain the future economic benefits and restrict others' access, normally via legal rights (though legal enforceability is not essential).
Research
Original and planned investigation undertaken with the prospect of gaining new scientific or technical knowledge and understanding.
Development
Application of research findings / other knowledge to a plan or design for new or substantially improved materials, devices, products, processes, systems or services before commercial production or use.
Active market
Market where items traded are homogeneous, willing buyers and sellers can normally be found at any time, and prices are publicly available.
Impairment loss / Carrying amount
Impairment loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount; carrying amount = amount recognised net of accumulated amortisation and impairment losses.

How It Works / Logic

  • Recognition criteria: recognise an intangible if, and only if, (a) it is probable that attributable future economic benefits will flow to the enterprise, assessed using reasonable, supportable assumptions; and (b) cost can be measured reliably. Measured initially at cost.
  • Separate acquisition: cost = purchase price + import duties and non-refundable taxes + directly attributable expenditure to make it ready for use (e.g. professional/legal fees), less trade discounts and rebates. If acquired for shares/securities, record at fair value of the asset or the securities, whichever is more clearly evident.
  • Acquisition in an amalgamation (nature of purchase, AS 14): recognise an intangible meeting the criteria even if not recognised by the transferor; if fair value cannot be measured reliably, include it in goodwill. Fair value: quoted market price (current bid) in an active market is most reliable; else price of most recent similar transaction; if no active market, the amount that would have been paid at arm's length — restricted so as not to create/increase a capital reserve.
  • Government grant: an intangible acquired free or for nominal consideration (e.g. landing rights, broadcast licences, import quotas) is recognised at nominal value or acquisition cost per AS 12, plus directly attributable cost to ready it for use.
  • Exchange of assets: cost measured at fair value (per AS 10) unless the exchange lacks commercial substance or fair value of neither asset is reliably measurable; otherwise at the carrying amount of the asset given up.
  • Internally generated goodwill: never recognised — not an identifiable resource measurable reliably at cost.
  • Internally generated intangibles: classify generation into a research phase and a development phase; if they cannot be distinguished, treat all as research. Research phase expenditure is always expensed when incurred. Development phase is capitalised only if the enterprise can demonstrate all six: (a) technical feasibility of completion; (b) intention to complete and use/sell; (c) ability to use/sell; (d) how it will generate probable future economic benefits (existence of a market or internal usefulness); (e) availability of adequate technical, financial and other resources; and (f) ability to measure attributable expenditure reliably.
  • Cost of internally generated intangible: sum of expenditure from the date the recognition criteria are first met (reinstatement of previously expensed amounts is prohibited); includes materials/services, employee costs of personnel directly engaged, directly attributable costs (e.g. legal registration fees, borrowing cost per AS 16) and reasonably allocable overheads. Excludes (expensed): selling/administrative/general overheads not directly attributable, identified inefficiencies and initial operating losses, and staff-training costs. Internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists are not recognised.
  • Recognition of an expense: expenditure on an intangible item is expensed when incurred unless it forms part of a qualifying intangible's cost or is acquired in an amalgamation and included in goodwill. Always expensed: research, start-up costs (unless within AS 10 fixed-asset cost), training, advertising/promotion, and relocation/reorganisation. Prepayments for goods/services are recognised as assets. Amounts once expensed cannot later be capitalised.
  • Subsequent expenditure: expensed when incurred unless it is probable it will enable benefits in excess of the originally assessed standard of performance and can be measured and attributed reliably; then added to cost. Subsequent expenditure on brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists is always expensed. Only rarely will post-recognition expenditure be capitalised.
  • Subsequent measurement: cost less accumulated amortisation and accumulated impairment losses.

Examples

  • Staff training (Example 1 — Moon Ltd): not an intangible — the enterprise cannot control benefits as staff may resign.
  • Production process: only the ₹1 lac incurred after criteria met on 1 Dec 20X1 is capitalised; the ₹9 lacs earlier is expensed permanently; at end 20X2 cost ₹21 lacs is impaired to recoverable ₹19 lacs (loss ₹2 lacs), reversible under AS 28.
  • Hydro-electric right (60 yrs) / toll motorway (30 yrs): amortised over the finite legal period unless shorter useful life; copyright for 10 yrs written off early when a new law invalidates the product.
  • Know-how (Illustration 1): ₹20 lakh over 10 yrs, not amortised for 7 yrs ⇒ Year-end entry credits Know-how ₹14,00,000, debiting P&L (prior period) ₹12,00,000 and current Amortisation ₹2,00,000.
  • Advertising (Illustration 3): ₹2 crore special campaign correctly expensed even though ₹25 crore turnover expected — no recognisable asset.
  • Software costs: costs before technological feasibility expensed (₹90,000); costs after until product costs capitalised (₹1,00,500); packing ₹16,500 expensed.

Why It Matters / Application

Amortisation: allocate the depreciable amount systematically over the best estimate of useful life; begin when the asset is available for use. AS 26 adopts a rebuttable presumption that useful life does not exceed ten years from availability for use. The method (straight-line, diminishing balance, or unit-of-production) reflects the pattern of consumption of economic benefits; if the pattern cannot be determined reliably, use straight-line. The amortisation charge is normally an expense (unless another standard requires capitalising it, e.g. into inventory cost). Residual value is assumed zero unless a third party commits to purchase, or an active market exists (residual value determinable and likely to still exist at end of useful life). Amortisation period and method are reviewed at least each financial year-end (changes accounted per AS 5).

Note

Useful life is always finite and is the shorter of the economic and legal periods; where control rests on finite legal rights, useful life cannot exceed that period unless renewal is virtually certain. Recoverability is tested per AS 28; recoverable amount of an intangible not yet available for use, or one amortised over more than ten years, must be estimated at least annually even without impairment indications. Retirement/disposal: derecognise on disposal or when no future benefits are expected; gain/loss = net disposal proceeds − carrying amount, recognised in P&L; an asset retired from active use and held for disposal is carried at its carrying amount at retirement. Disclosure (per class, distinguishing internally generated from others): useful lives / amortisation rates, methods, gross carrying amount and accumulated amortisation (with impairment) at start and end, and a reconciliation of carrying amount (additions — separately internal development and amalgamation; retirements/disposals; impairment losses and reversals; amortisation; other changes). Also disclose reasons where useful life exceeds ten years, material individual intangibles, restricted-title/pledged intangibles, acquisition commitments, and aggregate R&D expenditure recognised as expense.

Chapter 5 Unit 7 — AS 28: Impairment of Assets

Source: 87708bos-aps2158-ch5u7.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 28 prescribes procedures to ensure assets are carried at no more than their recoverable amount (the amount recoverable through use or sale), and sets out reversal principles and disclosures.
  • At each balance sheet date the enterprise assesses whether any indication of impairment exists; if so, it estimates recoverable amount. An asset is impaired when carrying amount > recoverable amount; the loss is recognised in P&L. The requirement applies equally to an individual asset or a cash-generating unit.
  • Scope excludes: inventories (AS 2), construction-contract assets (AS 7), financial assets/investments (AS 13), and deferred tax assets (AS 22) — other standards already address their impairment.
  • Applicability: mandatory from 1-4-2004 for listed enterprises (and those issuing listed securities) and other commercial/industrial/business enterprises with turnover exceeding ₹50 crores; from 1-4-2005 for all other enterprises.

Key Definitions / Terms

Recoverable amount
The higher of an asset's net selling price and its value in use.
Net selling price
Amount obtainable from sale in an arm's length transaction between knowledgeable, willing parties, less costs of disposal. Best evidence: price in a binding sale agreement; else active-market price; else best available information.
Costs of disposal
Incremental costs directly attributable to disposal, excluding finance costs and income-tax expense.
Value in use
Present value of estimated future cash flows from continuing use of the asset and from its disposal at the end of useful life (estimate cash flows, then apply an appropriate discount rate).
Impairment loss
Amount by which carrying amount exceeds recoverable amount.
Carrying amount
Amount recognised after deducting accumulated depreciation/amortisation and accumulated impairment losses.
Cash-generating unit (CGU)
Smallest identifiable group of assets generating cash inflows from continuing use that are largely independent of inflows from other assets or groups.
Corporate assets
Assets (e.g. headquarters/division building, EDP equipment, research centre) that do not generate independent cash inflows and whose carrying amount cannot be fully attributed to a CGU under review.

How It Works / Logic

  • Indicators (non-exhaustive): External — significant decline in market value beyond normal; adverse technological/market/economic/legal changes; increases in market interest rates/return raising the discount rate; carrying amount of net assets exceeding market capitalisation. Internal — obsolescence or physical damage; adverse changes in extent/manner of use (including plans to discontinue/restructure or dispose early); internal evidence of worse-than-expected economic performance (e.g. actual/budgeted cash flows or profit significantly worse than budgeted).
  • Estimating future cash flows: base on most recent approved budgets/forecasts for a maximum of five years (longer only if reliably demonstrable); extrapolate beyond using a steady or declining growth rate not exceeding long-term average growth (unless justified). Include net inflows from continuing use, directly attributable/allocable outflows to generate them, and net disposal cash flows. Exclude: inflows from largely-independent assets, outflows for already-recognised liabilities, restructuring inflows/outflows (until committed), future capacity-enhancing capital expenditure and its related savings, financing flows and income-tax receipts/payments. Foreign-currency flows are estimated in their own currency, discounted at a rate for that currency, then translated per AS 11.
  • Discount rate: a pre-tax rate reflecting current market assessments of the time value of money and risks specific to the asset, without double-counting risks already adjusted in the cash flows; when no asset-specific market rate exists, use incremental borrowing rate, CAPM, etc. Normally a single rate; separate rates for different periods where value in use is sensitive to differing period risks or term structure.
  • Recognition & measurement: if recoverable amount > carrying amount, no loss (but review remaining useful life, method and residual value per AS 10). If recoverable < carrying amount, the difference is an impairment loss, written off immediately to P&L; if the asset is carried at revalued figures, the loss is adjusted first against the revaluation surplus for that asset, and any balance to P&L. Recompute future depreciation on the new carrying amount, residual value and remaining useful life (AS 10). If the loss exceeds the asset's carrying amount, recognise a liability only if another standard requires it.
  • CGU identification: if recoverable amount of an individual asset cannot be determined (value in use undeterminable and likely differs from scrap value), use the CGU to which it belongs. If output has an active market, the asset/group is a separate CGU even if output is used internally; adjust internal transfer prices to management's best estimate of future market prices. Identify CGUs consistently period to period.
  • Carrying amount of a CGU: sum of carrying amounts of assets in the unit, determined consistently with how recoverable amount is determined; include a liability only if necessary (e.g. buyer must assume it), deducting it from both value in use and carrying amount.
  • Goodwill: cannot be tested alone (no independent cash flows) — test the CGU to which it belongs. If goodwill is allocable on a reasonable and consistent basis, apply the bottom-up test only; if not, apply both bottom-up and top-down tests.
  • Corporate assets: same rule — if allocable to the CGU reasonably, bottom-up only; if not, both bottom-up and top-down.
  • Impairment loss for a CGU: allocate first to goodwill, then pro-rata to other assets on carrying amount; but do not reduce any asset below the highest of its net selling price (if determinable), value in use (if determinable), and zero — reallocate any excess to other assets pro-rata. Recognise a liability for any remaining loss only if another standard requires.
  • Reversal: at each balance sheet date assess whether a prior impairment loss may no longer exist or has decreased (indicators mirror the impairment indicators). Reverse if estimates of cash inflows/outflows or discount rates have changed since the last loss — increase carrying amount to recoverable amount, but not above the carrying amount (net of depreciation/amortisation) that would have existed had no impairment been recognised. A loss written to P&L is reversed as income; one set against revaluation reserve is written back to that reserve (surplus to revenue). Adjust future depreciation to the revised carrying amount over remaining useful life.
  • CGU reversal order: allocate first to assets other than goodwill pro-rata on carrying amount, then to goodwill; subject to the same cap.
  • Goodwill reversal: generally not permitted for changes in estimates; reverse only if the loss was caused by a specific external event of an exceptional nature not expected to recur, and subsequent external events reverse that effect.

  • Private railway (Example 1) / bus routes (Example 2): CGU is the mine as a whole / the bus company as a whole, since individual assets/routes do not generate largely-independent inflows.

Subsequent negotiations or binding sale agreements may indicate further impairment or a decrease in prior losses.

Note

Chapter 6 Unit 1 — AS 15: Employee Benefits

Source: 87709bos-aps2158-ch6u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 15 prescribes the accounting treatment and disclosure, in the books of the employer, for all forms of consideration given in exchange for employee services — except employee share-based payments. It does not deal with accounting or reporting by employee benefit plans themselves.
  • Four things are clear at the outset: it applies to all employees (full-time, part-time, casual, temporary, whole-time directors and management); benefits may be paid in cash or in kind; benefits extend to employees and their dependents (spouse, children); and payment may be made directly to employees, dependents or third parties (legal heirs, nominees, insurers, trusts).
  • Founded on the matching premise: costs of employee benefits are matched with the timing of the service (e.g. pension is recognised as a cost during the service period, though paid after service ends).
  • The Standard does not define "employee"; it applies to all forms of employer-employee relationship without needing a formal contract. A "contract of service" (employee) differs from a "contract for services" (outsourced/professional). Benefits arising from informal practices are covered where the enterprise has no realistic alternative but to pay (an established, projectable pattern of benefits).
  • Four categories, each with separate recognition, measurement and disclosure rules: short-term benefits, post-employment benefits, other long-term benefits, and termination benefits. Applies in full to Level 1 enterprises from 1 April 2006; relaxations for others depending on whether they employ 50+ employees.

Key Definitions / Terms

Short-term benefits
Benefits (other than termination) falling due wholly within twelve months after the period service is rendered — wages, salaries, social security contributions, paid annual/sick leave, profit-sharing/bonus payable within 12 months, and non-monetary benefits (medical, housing, cars).
Post-employment benefits
Benefits payable after completion of employment — gratuity, pension, provident fund, post-employment medical care and life insurance.
Defined contribution plan (DCP)
Plan with fixed contributions into a separate fund and no further obligation; actuarial risk (benefits less than expected) and investment risk (assets insufficient) fall on the employee (e.g. Provident Fund).
Defined benefit plan (DBP)
Any post-employment plan other than a DCP; actuarial and investment risk fall on the employer, requiring detailed actuarial calculation.
Other long-term benefits
Long-service/sabbatical leave, jubilee benefits, long-term disability, and profit-sharing/bonuses/deferred compensation payable 12 months or more after service.
Termination benefits
Benefits payable on employer-initiated termination before normal retirement, or on an employee's acceptance of voluntary redundancy (e.g. VRS).

How It Works / Logic

  • Short-term benefits: recognise the undiscounted amount as an expense (unless another standard permits capitalisation); the unpaid balance is a liability, any excess paid is a prepayment (asset).
  • Compensated absences: accumulating (carried forward) — may be vesting (cash paid for unused entitlement on leaving) or non-vesting (no cash on leaving); recognised when service increases entitlement, measured as the additional amount expected to be paid for accumulated unused entitlement. No distinction between vesting/non-vesting except that non-vesting measurement allows for employees leaving before receiving. Non-accumulating (e.g. maternity leave) are recognised only when the absence occurs. Classification as short/long-term depends on both "falls due" and "expected to occur" within twelve months, judged on the overall behavioural pattern of all employees.
  • Profit-sharing/bonus recognised only if a present obligation exists from past events and a reliable estimate can be made — the latter needs a formula in the plan.
  • Post-employment: for DCP the contribution is charged to income. An insured plan is treated as a DCP unless the enterprise remains obliged to pay benefits directly or to make good if the insurer fails. Insured funds may be plan assets (netted against the obligation) or reimbursement rights (obligation and right shown separately, inflating both sides of the balance sheet); both are fair-valued.
  • DBP balance sheet liability = present value of the obligation − unrecognised past service cost − fair value of plan assets. A resulting net asset is capped at the lower of that amount and the present value of available economic benefits (refunds or reduced future contributions).
  • Seven cost components recognised in P&L (Para 61): current service cost; interest cost; expected return on plan assets (and reimbursement rights); actuarial gains/losses (as recognised); past service cost (as recognised); effect of curtailments/settlements; and the excess of the negative net amount over Para 59(b). A settlement eliminates further obligations (e.g. commuted pension); a curtailment reduces covered employees or benefits. Gains/losses on settlement or curtailment are recognised when they occur.
  • Actuarial assumptions must be unbiased, mutually compatible best estimates — neither imprudent nor excessively conservative — comprising demographic (mortality, turnover, disability, early retirement, dependents, claim rates) and financial (discount rate, future salary/benefit levels, future medical costs, expected return on plan assets) assumptions. Financial assumptions are based on market expectations at the balance sheet date. Actuarial gains/losses (experience adjustments plus effects of changed assumptions) are recognised immediately in P&L.
  • Termination benefits are recognised as a liability and expense only when there is a detailed, duly approved formal plan and a reliable estimate; amounts due within 12 months are undiscounted, those beyond 12 months are discounted. Voluntary redundancy is measured by the number expected to accept.

  • Salary accrual (Illus. 3): Salary ₹2 crore, ₹1.90 crore paid to 31 March; expense ₹2 crore, liability ₹10 lakhs.
  • Accumulating leave (Ex.): 100 employees, 5 days each, carried forward 1 year (LIFO); 8 employees expected to take 1½ extra days each → liability of 12 days of pay.
  • Profit-sharing (Ex.): Plan pays 3% of net profit; turnover reduces it to 2.5% — recognise liability/expense of 2.5%.
  • Actuarial surplus (Illus. 6): ₹6 lakh surplus must be credited to P&L immediately, not spread over 2 years.
  • Return on plan assets (Illus. 7): Expected return ₹11,750 (10.25% on ₹1,00,000 + 5% on ₹30,000 for 6 months); actual return ₹20,000.
  • Curtailment (Illus. 8): Reduction in gross obligation ₹600 less proportionate unamortised past service cost ₹18 = gain ₹582; liability after curtailment ₹138.
  • DBO (Illus. 9): Expected last salary ₹24,00,000; DBO = ₹24,00,000 × 25% × 5 = ₹30,00,000, charged ₹6,00,000 p.a. with current service cost discounted at 8% plus interest cost.

Chapter 6 Unit 2 — AS 29 (Revised): Provisions, Contingent Liabilities & Contingent Assets

Source: 87710bos-aps2158-ch6u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Effective for accounting periods on or after 1 April 2004; prescribes recognition, measurement and disclosure of provisions and contingent liabilities, and lays down accounting for contingent assets, so users understand their nature, timing and amount.
  • Aims to stop "profit smoothing" — creating arbitrary provisions in high-profit years (to reduce profit/tax) and reversing them in lean years (to inflate profit/EPS) — by fixing valid recognition criteria, defining the roles of management (estimate) and auditors (vouch), and requiring transactions qualifying for a provision to be recognised as a balance-sheet liability.
  • Provisions affect both the P&L (an expense) and the balance sheet (a liability); AS 29 promotes transparency and rules out manipulation.
  • Scope excludes items from fair-valued financial instruments, executory contracts (unless onerous), insurer policyholder contracts, and items covered by another standard (AS 7, 9, 15, 19, 22) — the specific standard is applied instead.

Key Definitions / Terms

Executory contracts
Contracts under which neither party has performed, or both have partially performed to an equal extent.
Provision
A liability that can be measured only by using a substantial degree of estimation.
Liability
A present obligation from past events whose settlement is expected to result in an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits.
Obligating event
An event creating an obligation the enterprise has no realistic alternative but to settle.
Contingent liability
A possible obligation confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within control; or a present obligation not recognised because outflow is not probable or no reliable estimate can be made.
Contingent asset
A possible asset from past events confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within control; not recognised.
Present / possible obligation
Present = existence at balance sheet date is probable (more likely than not); possible = existence is not probable.
Restructuring
A management-planned and controlled programme materially changing the scope of a business or the manner in which it is conducted.

How It Works / Logic

  • Recognise a provision only when all three are met: (a) a present obligation exists from a past event; (b) outflow of resources is probable (probability of occurring greater than not); and (c) a reliable estimate of the amount can be made. If any is not met, no provision.
  • Present obligation: determined from all available evidence (including experts). If more likely than not that it exists, provide; if more likely that none exists, disclose a contingent liability unless outflow is remote.
  • Past event: only obligations existing independently of the enterprise's future actions qualify (e.g. penalties, clean-up of unlawful damage, decommissioning of an oil rig for damage already caused). No provision for costs the enterprise can avoid by future action (e.g. fitting smoke filters). A new law can turn earlier damage into an obligating event; a proposed law counts only when virtually certain to be enacted.
  • Probable outflow: where there are many similar obligations (e.g. warranties), probability is judged for the class as a whole, so a provision may arise even if any single item is unlikely.
  • Reliable estimate: use of estimates is inherent; a range of outcomes can almost always be determined. In the extremely rare case no reliable estimate is possible, the liability is disclosed as a contingent liability instead.
  • Contingent liabilities are disclosed (not recognised) unless outflow is remote; the part of a joint-and-several obligation expected to be met by others is a contingent liability. Contingent liabilities are re-assessed continually — if outflow becomes probable, a provision is recognised in that period.
  • Contingent assets are not recognised (income may never be realised) and not disclosed in the financial statements — disclosed only in the approving authority's report when inflow is probable; recognised only when realisation becomes virtually certain.
  • Measurement is the best estimate of expenditure to settle at the balance sheet date, based on management judgment, experience and, where needed, expert reports. Not discounted, except decommissioning/restoration liabilities recognised as cost of PPE, using a pre-tax rate reflecting time value and risks specific to the liability; the unwinding of discount goes to P&L. Provisions are measured before tax (tax effects under AS 22).
  • Risks, uncertainties and future events (with sufficient objective evidence) are reflected in the estimate; gains on expected disposal of assets are not offset against a provision. Provisions are reviewed each balance sheet date and reversed if outflow is no longer probable; a provision is used only for the expenditure for which it was originally recognised.
  • No provision for future operating losses. Restructuring provisions arise only when the recognition criteria are met (e.g. a binding sale agreement) and include only direct expenditure necessarily entailed and not associated with ongoing activities — excluding retraining/relocating staff, marketing, and new-system investment.

Examples

  • Warranty (Ex. 3): A 6-month free-repair warranty creates a present obligation, so a provision is required.
  • Restoration (Ex. 4): Lease requiring the building be vacated in original condition (₹10 lakhs at year 10) — provide the present value of the cost.
  • Lawsuits (Illus. 1): Loss not probable/remote — disclose expected loss ₹10,40,000 (₹68,000×10 + ₹72,000×5) as a contingent liability, not a provision.
  • Onerous contract (Q6): Likely failure to deliver goods — provide the full compensation of ₹1.50 crore, not net of profit (no profit accrued as goods unmade).
  • Warranty computation (Q8): Provision ₹2,400 at 31.3.20X1 and ₹4,850 at 31.3.20X2; ₹2,450 debited to P&L; no provision on the invoice whose 2-year warranty has expired.

Chapter 7 Unit 1 — AS 4: Contingencies & Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date

Source: 87711bos-aps2158-ch7u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 4 (Revised) deals with the treatment and disclosure of events occurring after the balance sheet date. Its contingency paragraphs apply only to the extent not covered by other standards; e.g. impairment of financial assets such as receivables (provision for bad and doubtful debts) is still governed here.
  • Following AS 29 becoming mandatory (periods on/after 1 April 2004), the contingency paragraphs of AS 4 stand withdrawn except where they deal with impairment of assets not covered by any other standard (per Companies (Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules, 2016).

Key Definitions / Terms

Contingency
A condition or situation whose ultimate outcome (gain or loss) will be known only on the occurrence or non-occurrence of one or more uncertain future events.
Events occurring after the balance sheet date
Significant favourable or unfavourable events occurring between the balance sheet date and the date the financial statements are approved by the Board (or corresponding authority).
Adjusting event
An event providing further evidence of conditions that existed at the balance sheet date (e.g. a trade receivable declared insolvent after year-end against whom a provision was made).
Non-adjusting event
An event indicative of conditions that arose after the balance sheet date (e.g. plant damaged by fire after year-end).

How It Works / Logic

  • Contingent losses: if likely (probable) to result in a loss, it is prudent to provide; the amount is based on management judgment using information up to the approval date, experience and expert reports. Where evidence is conflicting or insufficient to estimate the amount, disclose the existence and nature. Guarantees, discounted bills and similar obligations are generally disclosed by note even if the loss possibility is remote.
  • Contingent gains are never recognised (recognition could bring in revenue never realised); when realisation becomes virtually certain, the item is no longer a contingency and may be recognised.
  • Adjusting events require adjustment to the reported amounts of assets and liabilities (e.g. insolvency of a customer confirming a bad debt existing at year-end).
  • Non-adjusting events (e.g. ordinary decline in market value of investments, fire destroying a plant after year-end) do not adjust the figures; if material, disclosed in the approving authority's report to enable proper user evaluations.
  • Proposed dividends declared after the balance sheet date are not recognised as a liability (no obligation exists at year-end unless a statute requires) but are disclosed in the notes per the Companies (Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules, 2016 — applicable from FY ended 31 March 2017 onward.
  • If events after year-end indicate the going concern assumption is no longer valid (deterioration, destruction of the substratum), the statements are prepared on a liquidation basis.

Examples

  • Theft (Illus. 1): Cash theft of ₹5 lakhs during the period, detected before approval — recognise the loss and adjust (adjusting event).
  • Earthquake (Illus. 2): Warehouse destroyed after year-end (₹30 lakhs) is non-adjusting; disclose in the approving authority's report and evaluate going concern.
  • Rocky surface (Illus. 5): Extra cost of ₹80 lakhs becoming evident after the accounts were approved is not an event occurring after the balance sheet date.
  • Cheques in hand (Ex.): Cheques dated 31 March but received after year-end do not reflect a condition at year-end and are not controlled at year-end — recognising them as an asset is inconsistent with AS 4.
  • Court decision (Q6): Judgment for ₹14 lakhs delivered before approval on a matter already provided at ₹10 lakhs is an adjusting event — raise the provision by ₹4 lakhs; had the judgment come after approval, no adjustment.

Worked example — adjusting vs non-adjusting (Q6). Year-end 31 March 20X1; accounts approved by the Board on 15 June 20X1. A provision of ₹10,00,000 was carried at year-end for a pending lawsuit.

  1. Adjusting case: the court delivers judgment of ₹14,00,000 on 20 May 20X1 (before approval). This gives further evidence of a condition existing at 31 March 20X1, so the provision is increased by ₹14,00,000 − ₹10,00,000 = ₹4,00,000, and the liability in the balance sheet is restated to ₹14,00,000.
  2. Non-adjusting case: had the same ₹14,00,000 judgment been delivered on 30 June 20X1 (after approval), the 20X1 figures are not adjusted; the ₹10,00,000 provision stands and the shortfall is dealt with in the next period.

Why It Matters / Application

Disclosure of events after the balance sheet date requires the nature of the event and an estimate of its financial effect (or a statement that none can be made), aiding proper user evaluation. Substance over form applies — e.g. a building sold with possession given but legal formalities pending should be recorded as a sale, recognising the gain.

Note

AS 4 does not apply to obligations under retirement benefit plans, commitments from long-term lease contracts, or liabilities of life-assurance and general-insurance enterprises arising from issued policies.

Chapter 7 Unit 2 — AS 5: Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items & Changes in Accounting Policies

Source: 87712bos-aps2158-ch7u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 5 prescribes the classification and disclosure of certain items in the statement of profit and loss so all enterprises present it on a uniform basis, enhancing comparability over time and across enterprises.
  • It requires classification/disclosure of extraordinary and prior period items, disclosure of certain items within ordinary activities, and specifies the treatment of changes in accounting estimates and disclosures for changes in accounting policies.
  • It does not deal with the tax implications of extraordinary items, prior period items, or changes in estimates/policies — appropriate adjustments are made per circumstances.

Key Definitions / Terms

Ordinary activities
Activities undertaken as part of business and related activities incidental to, in furtherance of, or arising from them (e.g. profit on sale of merchandise).
Extraordinary items
Income/expenses from events or transactions clearly distinct from ordinary activities and not expected to recur frequently or regularly (e.g. earthquake loss, attachment of property). Judged by nature relative to the enterprise's ordinary business, not by frequency — so an item may be extraordinary for one enterprise but not another.
Exceptional items
Not a term under AS 5 or Schedule III; used for understanding — large/relevant ordinary items disclosed separately (e.g. inventory write-downs and reversals, restructuring, disposals of PPE/long-term investments, retrospective legislative changes, litigation settlements, reversals of provisions).
Prior period items
Income/expenses arising in the current period from errors or omissions in prior-period financial statements (mathematical mistakes, wrong application of policies, misinterpretation, oversight).
Change in accounting estimate
Revision of an approximation due to new information, more experience or subsequent developments; by nature not an extraordinary or prior period item.
Accounting policies
Specific accounting principles and methods of applying them adopted in preparing and presenting financial statements.

How It Works / Logic

  • All income/expense recognised in a period enters net profit/loss unless another standard requires otherwise. The net profit/loss comprises profit/loss from ordinary activities and extraordinary items, both disclosed on the face of the statement.
  • Extraordinary items are shown as part of net profit/loss for the period, with the nature and amount disclosed separately so their impact is perceivable.
  • Prior period items are normally included in the current net profit/loss (alternatively shown after determination of current profit); either way the effect on current profit must be perceivable. They are distinguished from changes in estimates (e.g. income on a previously unestimable contingency is not a prior period item).
  • A change in estimate is recognised in the period of change (if it affects only that period, e.g. bad debts) or in the period of change and future periods (if both, e.g. revised useful life), using the same P&L classification as previously (ordinary stays ordinary, extraordinary stays extraordinary). Material effects are disclosed; if impracticable to quantify, that fact is disclosed. Where a change cannot be distinguished as policy or estimate, treat it as a change in estimate.
  • Accounting policies changed only when required by statute, for compliance with a standard, or when it results in more appropriate presentation. Material changes and their adjustments are shown in the period of change; if the effect is not ascertainable, that fact is indicated; if immaterial now but expected material later, the fact of change is disclosed. Changes on adopting a standard follow that standard's transitional provisions.
  • Not changes in policy: adopting a policy for events/transactions differing in substance from previous ones (e.g. a formal gratuity scheme replacing ad hoc ex-gratia payments); or adopting a new policy for events/transactions that did not occur previously or were immaterial.

Examples

  • Old creditor write-off (Illus.): Writing off a 5-year-old sundry creditor of ₹50,000 is not a prior period item (no prior error/omission).
  • Fuel surcharge (Illus. 1): A final bill paid in Feb 20X8 but accounted in 20X8-X9 is a prior period item; it is an ordinary (not extraordinary) expense.
  • Wage revision (Illus. 2): Retrospective wage revision agreed after year-end — additional ₹7,50,000 included in current year's wages, not a prior period item; disclosed if size/nature relevant.
  • Inventory omission (Illus. 3): Two omitted inventory pages worth ₹14.5 lakhs is a prior period item requiring separate disclosure.
  • Depreciation method (Illus. 5): Change from SLM to WDV is a change in accounting estimate, not an extraordinary item; the deficiency is a normal expense.
  • GST demand (Q6): GST paid on ordinary consultancy services is an ordinary-activity item, not extraordinary.

Worked example — prior period item vs current-period estimate (Illus. 1 & 3). Consider two items surfacing in 20X8-X9:

  1. Prior period item (error): while closing 20X8-X9, it is found that two pages of the closing inventory sheet for 20X7-X8 — worth ₹14,50,000 — were omitted, understating that year's profit. Because it arises from an omission in a prior period's statements, the ₹14,50,000 correction is a prior period item: it is included in the current net profit but its nature and amount are disclosed separately, so its effect on current profit is perceivable (it is not extraordinary).
  2. Not a prior period item: a retrospective wage revision agreed after year-end adds ₹7,50,000 to wages. This flows from a new agreement (not an error), so it is charged to the current year's ordinary wages, disclosed if its size or nature is relevant — no restatement of the earlier year.

Why It Matters / Application

Separate classification and disclosure of extraordinary items, prior period items and material ordinary items enhance the comparability of financial statements over time and across enterprises, and help users make projections.

Note

Under AS 10 (Revised), residual value and useful life of an asset are reviewed at least each year-end; a change is accounted for as a change in accounting estimate per AS 5.

Chapter 7 Unit 3 — AS 11: The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates

Source: 87713bos-aps2158-ch7u3.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • An enterprise may deal with foreign exchange in two ways: foreign currency transactions (e.g. export sales in USD) or foreign operations (e.g. a foreign branch). Both must be translated into the reporting currency (INR) for inclusion in the financial statements — deciding which rate to use and how to recognise the effects of rate changes.
  • Applies to: accounting for transactions in foreign currencies; translating the financial statements of foreign operations; and forward exchange contracts.
  • Does not: specify the presentation currency (though disclosure is required if a currency other than the domicile currency is used, and for any change); deal with cash-flow-statement presentation of foreign cash flows (AS 3); deal with exchange differences on borrowings treated as an interest adjustment; or deal with convenience restatement into another currency.

Key Definitions / Terms

Foreign currency transaction
A transaction denominated in or requiring settlement in a foreign currency — buying/selling goods or services, borrowing/lending, entering a forward contract, or acquiring/disposing of assets/liabilities in foreign currency.
Monetary items
Money held and assets/liabilities to be received or paid in fixed or determinable amounts of money (cash, receivables, payables).
Non-monetary items
Assets/liabilities other than monetary items (fixed assets, advances for purchases, inventories, equity investments).
Foreign operation
A subsidiary, associate, joint venture or branch whose activities are based in a country other than the reporting enterprise's.
Integral foreign operation
A foreign operation whose activities are an integral part of the reporting enterprise's — carried on as an extension of the enterprise.
Non-integral foreign operation
A foreign operation that is not integral; a rate change affects the enterprise's net investment rather than individual items.
Net investment / Forward contract / Forward rate / Foreign currency
Net investment = the enterprise's share in the net assets of the operation. Forward contract = agreement to exchange currencies at a forward rate. Forward rate = the specified rate for exchanging two currencies at a specified future date. Foreign currency = a currency other than the reporting currency.

How It Works / Logic

  • Initial recognition: record at the exchange rate between reporting and foreign currency on the transaction date (an average rate for a week/month may approximate, unless rates fluctuate significantly).
  • At each balance sheet date: (a) monetary items at the closing rate (or a more realistic realisable/disbursable amount where remittance is restricted or the closing rate is unrealistic); (b) non-monetary items at historical cost use the transaction-date rate; (c) non-monetary items at fair value use the rate when the value was determined; (d) foreign-currency contingent liabilities disclosed at the closing rate.
  • Exchange differences on monetary items (on settlement, or on reporting at a rate different from initial recording) are recognised as income/expense in the period they arise; within the same period the whole difference is recognised, across periods each intervening period's change is recognised.
  • Integral operations: translated as if all transactions were the enterprise's own — fixed assets/depreciation at the purchase-date (or valuation) rate, inventory cost at rates when incurred, recoverable/realisable amounts at the rate when determined (usually closing); resulting differences go to P&L.
  • Non-integral operations: all assets and liabilities (monetary and non-monetary) at the closing rate; income/expenses at transaction-date rates (average for practicality); goodwill/capital reserve and contingent liabilities at the closing rate; all resulting differences accumulated in a Foreign Currency Translation Reserve until disposal. Intra-group monetary items are not eliminated against other balances. On disposal, the cumulative deferred differences are recognised as income/expense; on partial disposal, only the proportionate share; a write-down is not a partial disposal.
  • Change in classification: integral → non-integral, differences on translating non-monetary assets at reclassification date go to the translation reserve; non-integral → integral, translated amounts at the date of change become the historical cost, and deferred differences are not recognised until disposal.
  • Para 46/46A option: exchange differences on long-term (12+ months) foreign-currency monetary items relating to a depreciable asset may be added to/deducted from the asset's cost and depreciated over its balance life; other cases may be accumulated in FCMITDA and amortised over the item's balance period. The Para 46A option is irrevocable and applies to all such items.
  • Forward exchange contracts (not for trading/speculation): the premium/discount at inception is amortised over the contract life; exchange differences go to P&L; profit/loss on cancellation or renewal is recognised in the period. Speculative contracts: premium/discount ignored, marked to market at each balance sheet date with gain/loss recognised.

  • Foreign loan (Illus. 3): $4,50,000 borrowed at ₹48, reported at ₹49 (loss ₹4,50,000) and repaid at ₹49.50 (further loss ₹2,25,000).
  • Forward contract (Illus. 4): Premium ₹0.30 × $1,00,000 = ₹30,000 loss; ₹20,000 in the current year (2 of 3 months), ₹10,000 next.

Chapter 7 Unit 4 — AS 22: Accounting for Taxes on Income

Source: 87714bos-aps2158-ch7u4.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 22 prescribes the accounting treatment of taxes on income following the matching concept — matching tax expense against the revenue of the period.
  • The divergence between taxable income and accounting income arises for two reasons: differences between items treated as revenue/expense/deduction for accounting versus tax; and differences in the amount recognised for a particular item in P&L versus for taxable-income computation.
  • These differences split into permanent differences (never reverse) and timing differences (originate and reverse). Only timing differences give rise to deferred tax.

Key Definitions / Terms

Accounting income (loss)
Net profit/loss per the statement of profit and loss before deducting income-tax expense or adding tax saving.
Taxable income (tax loss)
Income/loss for a period determined under tax laws, on which income-tax payable/recoverable is determined.
Tax expense (tax saving)
Aggregate of current tax and deferred tax charged/credited to P&L (Current Tax + Deferred Tax = Tax expense).
Current tax
Income tax determined to be payable (recoverable) on the taxable income (tax loss) for a period.
Deferred tax
The tax effect of timing differences.
Timing differences
Differences between taxable and accounting income that originate in one period and are capable of reversal in one or more subsequent periods (e.g. depreciation, scientific-research assets fully deductible in year one).
Permanent differences
Differences that originate but do not reverse subsequently (e.g. a disallowed fine); they increase current tax but create no deferred tax.

How It Works / Logic

  • Tax expense (current + deferred) is included in the net profit/loss for the period; the tax effects of timing differences appear as deferred tax assets (DTA) or liabilities (DTL) in the balance sheet.
  • Prudence: DTAs are recognised and carried forward only where there is reasonable certainty of realisation (examined via past record and realistic future profit estimates). Where there is unabsorbed depreciation or carried-forward losses, DTAs are recognised only where there is virtual certainty supported by convincing evidence of sufficient future taxable income. Permanent differences never create deferred tax.
  • Measurement: current tax at the amount expected to be paid/recovered using applicable rates and laws; deferred tax at rates/laws enacted (or substantively enacted, where announcements have that effect) by the balance sheet date. Deferred tax is not discounted.
  • Re-assessment: at each balance sheet date, previously unrecognised DTAs are re-assessed and recognised to the extent it has become reasonably/virtually certain that sufficient future taxable income will be available.
  • Review: the carrying amount of DTAs is reviewed at each balance sheet date and written down to the extent it is no longer reasonably/virtually certain that sufficient income will be available; a write-down is reversed to the extent certainty returns.
  • Virtual certainty is a matter of judgment evaluated case-by-case — certainty for all practical purposes, supported by convincing evidence available in concrete form (e.g. a profitable binding export order), not mere forecasts, business plans or projections accepted by a credit agency.
  • Disclosure: no specific requirement to disclose current/deferred tax on the face of P&L (though the Companies Act requires income-tax disclosure), and no reconciliation of accounting profit to tax expense is required. In the balance sheet, DTA/DTL are broken into major components in the notes, distinguished from current tax, and shown under a separate heading. The nature of evidence supporting a DTA is disclosed where there is unabsorbed depreciation/carried-forward losses. Current tax is offset where there is a legally enforceable right and intent to settle net; deferred tax is offset where there is a legally enforceable right and the same governing tax law.
  • Relevant explanations: tax-holiday sections 80-IA/80-IB — deferred tax on timing differences reversing during the holiday period is not recognised (to the extent income is deducted), while those reversing after are recognised when originating; sections 10A/10B — similar treatment for differences originating during the holiday, with prudence for DTAs; both use first-originated-first-reversed. Section 115JB (MAT) payment is a current tax; deferred tax is measured at the regular tax rates, not the 115JB rate.

Examples

  • Transition (Illus. 1): Excess tax depreciation ₹3,00,000 gives DTL ₹1,50,000; unamortised preliminary expenses ₹30,000 give DTA ₹15,000; net DTL ₹1,35,000 (at 50%).
  • MAT (Illus. 2): DTL ₹1,08,000; tax debited to P&L ₹1,34,250 (current ₹12,000 + DTL ₹1,08,000 + MAT excess ₹14,250).
  • Carry-forward loss (Illus. 3): Loss ₹2,00,000 creates DTA ₹80,000, reversed (₹40,000 each) as profits arise in 20X2 and 20X3.
  • Percentage-of-completion (Illus. 4): DTL of ₹1,40,000 in 20X0-X1 reverses to nil by 20X2-X3 as timing differences unwind.
  • Tax holiday (Q6): Of ₹200 lakh year-1 difference, ₹80 lakh reverses in the holiday (not recognised); DTL ₹48 lakh recognised; year-2 ₹400 lakh reverses after holiday → DTL ₹160 lakh; balance ₹208 lakh.
  • Section 43B (Q8): GST ₹5,00,000 disallowed until paid gives a DTA ₹1,50,000 (at 30%).

Worked example — deferred tax on a depreciation timing difference (Illus. 1). Rama Ltd. reports: depreciation per accounting (books) ₹2,00,000; depreciation per income-tax records ₹5,00,000; and unamortised preliminary expenses per tax records ₹30,000 (fully written off in the books but allowed to the tax computation over years). There is adequate evidence of future taxable profit; tax rate 50%.

  3. Net position: DTL ₹1,50,000 − DTA ₹15,000 = net deferred tax liability ₹1,35,000, recognised as the transition adjustment.

Why It Matters / Application

Recognising deferred tax matches tax expense with the accounting period to which the related income and expenses relate, giving a truer measure of after-tax profit despite divergence between taxable and accounting income.

Chapter 8 Unit 1 — AS 7: Construction Contracts

Source: 87715bos-aps2158-ch8u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 7 prescribes accounting for construction contracts in the financial statements of contractors, determining when (timing) and how much (amount) contract revenue and contract costs are recognised in the statement of profit and loss.
  • A standard is needed because construction contracts (flyovers, dams, metro lines, buildings) are long-term — they span more than one accounting period, and the final outcome is known only after several years, so revenue and cost must be allocated to the periods in which work is performed (Work in Progress).
  • Requires two parties — the Contractor (who completes construction) and the Contractee/Customer. A self-constructed asset (e.g. own engineering division builds a machine for the production division) is outside AS 7 — it falls under AS 10 (Property, Plant and Equipment).
  • Scope also covers contracts for rendering services directly related to construction (project managers, architects) and contracts for destruction or restoration of assets and environmental restoration following demolition.

Key Definitions / Terms

Construction contract
A contract specifically negotiated for the construction of an asset or a combination of assets closely interrelated or interdependent in design, technology, function or ultimate purpose/use (e.g. bridge, dam, refinery, complex plant).
Fixed price contract
Contractor agrees a fixed contract price or fixed rate per unit of output, in some cases subject to cost-escalation clauses. Common in public tenders.
Cost-plus contract
Contractor is reimbursed allowable/defined costs plus a percentage of those costs or a fixed fee. Common where costs or completion time are uncertain.
Stage / percentage of completion method
Method recognising revenue and expenses by reference to the stage of completion of the contract at the reporting date.
Retentions
Progress billings not paid until conditions specified in the contract are satisfied or defects rectified.
Progress billings / Advances
Amounts billed for work performed (whether or not paid) / amounts received by the contractor before the related work is performed.

How It Works / Logic

  • Combining and segmenting: When a contract covers several assets, each asset is a separate contract if separate proposals were submitted, each was separately negotiated (able to be accepted/rejected), and costs and revenues of each are identifiable. A group of contracts is treated as a single contract if negotiated as one package, so closely interrelated as to be one project with an overall margin, and performed concurrently/continuously. An additional optional asset is a separate contract if it differs significantly in design/technology/function or its price is negotiated without regard to the original price.
  • Contract revenue = initial amount agreed + variations in contract work, claims and incentive payments (only to the extent probable and reliably measurable) + agreed cost escalation − penalties (usually for late completion). Claims are included only when it is probable the customer will accept and the amount is reliably measurable; incentives only when performance standards are probable and reliably measurable.
  • Contract costs = (i) costs directly relating to the specific contract (site labour and supervision, materials, depreciation of plant used, moving/hiring plant, design/technical assistance, estimated rectification/warranty, third-party claims), (ii) costs attributable to contract activity in general and allocable (insurance, non-specific design/technical assistance, construction overheads — allocated on normal activity levels; may include AS 16 borrowing costs), and (iii) costs specifically chargeable to the customer under the contract. Direct costs are reduced by incidental income not in contract revenue (e.g. sale of surplus material).
  • Excluded costs: general administration (unless reimbursement specified), selling costs, R&D (unless reimbursement specified), and depreciation of idle plant not used on the contract.
  • Percentage of completion method is used only when the outcome can be estimated reliably — consistent with accrual and matching. For a fixed price contract: total revenue measurable reliably; economic benefits probable; costs to complete and stage of completion measurable reliably; costs identifiable so actual can be compared with estimates. For a cost-plus contract: economic benefits probable; and costs attributable (whether or not reimbursable) identifiable and measurable reliably.
  • If the outcome cannot be estimated reliably: recognise revenue only to the extent of contract costs incurred of which recovery is probable, and expense contract costs as incurred. When uncertainties cease, revert to the percentage completion method.
  • An expected loss (total contract costs expected to exceed total contract revenue) is recognised as an expense immediately (prudence), regardless of stage of completion or whether work has commenced.
  • Costs relating to future activity are recognised as an asset (contract work in progress) if recovery is probable. Uncollectable contract revenue already recognised is expensed, not adjusted against revenue.
  • Stage of completion measured by: (a) proportion of costs incurred for work performed to estimated total costs; (b) surveys of work performed (surveyor's certification, common on government projects); or (c) completion of a physical proportion of the work (e.g. area tiled ÷ total area). Progress payments and advances need not reflect work performed.
  • Changes in estimates are treated as a change in accounting estimate under AS 5, applied cumulatively; changed estimates affect revenue/expenses in the period of change and later periods.

  3. Stage of completion (WIP) = ₹64,99,000 ÷ ₹97,00,000 = 67%; revenue = 67% × ₹85,00,000 = ₹56,95,000, so the loss booked via the stage method is ₹56,95,000 − ₹64,99,000 = (₹8,04,000), and the extra provision required = ₹12,00,000 − ₹8,04,000 = ₹3,96,000.

Why It Matters / Application

Recognising profit over the construction period matches revenue with associated costs in the same period and incorporates prudence (foreseeable losses recognised at once), giving a fair representation of long-term contract performance rather than deferring all profit to completion (which would show early-year losses followed by inflated profits).

Note

Gross amount due from/to customers = (Costs incurred + recognised profits − recognised losses) − progress billings (received + receivable). A positive figure is an asset (due from customers); a negative figure is a liability (due to customers). Disclosures also cover the amount of contract revenue recognised, methods used to determine it and to determine stage of completion, plus aggregate costs and recognised profits, advances received, and retentions.

Chapter 8 Unit 2 — AS 9: Revenue Recognition

Source: 87716bos-aps2158-ch8u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 9 deals with the bases for recognition of revenue in the statement of profit and loss, arising in the ordinary course of activities from: sale of goods, rendering of services, and use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends.
  • Mandatory for all enterprises. It does not deal with revenue from construction contracts, hire-purchase/lease agreements, government grants/subsidies, or insurance contracts (special considerations apply).
  • Items not "revenue": realised/unrealised gains on disposal or holding of non-current assets (e.g. appreciation of fixed assets), holding gains on current assets and natural increases in herds/agricultural produce, forex gains and translation adjustments, and gains from discharging or restating obligations.

Key Definitions / Terms

Revenue
Gross inflow of cash, receivables or other consideration from the sale of goods, the rendering of services, and the use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends, in the ordinary course of business.
Agency relationship
Where the entity acts as agent (risks and rewards rest with the principal), revenue is only the commission/fee earned — not the gross inflow.
Proportionate completion method
Service revenue recognised proportionately by reference to the performance of each act, where performance consists of more than one act.
Completed service contract method
Revenue recognised only when the rendering of services is completed or substantially completed (a single act, or where remaining acts are so significant that performance is not complete until they are executed).
Interest / Royalties / Dividends
Charges for use of cash resources / charges for use of assets such as know-how, patents, trademarks, copyrights / rewards from holding investments in shares.

How It Works / Logic

  • Sale of goods — timing: performance is achieved when (i) the seller has transferred property in the goods for a price, or all significant risks and rewards of ownership have transferred and the seller retains no effective control, and (ii) no significant uncertainty exists over the amount of consideration. Where risks/rewards pass at a time different from title, revenue is recognised on transfer of risks and rewards. All this is provided ultimate collection is not unreasonable to expect at the time of performance; otherwise recognition is postponed.
  • Rendering of services: usually recognised as the service is performed, via the proportionate completion method or the completed service contract method (practically, proportionate completion is often on a straight-line basis).
  • Use of enterprise resources: Interest — time-proportion basis (amount outstanding × rate); Royalties — accrual basis per the agreement terms; Dividends — when the owner's right to receive payment is established. Recognised only when no significant uncertainty as to measurability or collectability exists.
  • Agency: the entity determines whether it is principal (obligation to provide the goods/services itself) or agent (arranges for another to provide them); an agent recognises only its commission/fee.
  • Special cases for sale of goods: (1) Delivery delayed at buyer's request (bill-and-hold) — recognise if goods are identified, cannot be diverted, delay is at buyer's request and goods are ready. (2) Sale on approval — recognise only on formal acceptance, an act adopting the transaction, or expiry of the rejection period. (3) Goods subject to inspection/installation — if installation is complex/significant, recognise after satisfactory installation; if simple, recognise on agreement to purchase. (4) Sale and repurchase — treated as a financing arrangement (borrowing), no sale recognised. (5) Trade discounts and volume rebates — deducted in determining revenue (and not part of revenue when received). (6) Cash discounts — not deducted; revenue is gross and the discount is an expense. (7) Consignment sale — recognise only when goods are sold to a third party.
  • Effect of uncertainties: where ability to assess ultimate collection with reasonable certainty is lacking at the time of raising a claim, recognition is postponed to the extent of the uncertainty. When uncertainty arises after the sale/service, make a separate provision rather than adjust the original revenue. If consideration is not reasonably determinable, recognition is postponed. Postponed revenue is recognised in the period it becomes certain to be collected.

Examples

  • Illustration 1 (Zigato): As delivery agent it recognises only ₹60 lakh delivery revenue; restaurants (principal) recognise ₹200 lakh; GST ₹40 lakh is a liability to government, not revenue.
  • Illustration 3 (AB–CD): Sale ₹2 lakh recognised on 1 Jan 20X1 (collection reasonable at time of sale); on later liquidation, receivable is written off as bad debts — not a reversal of revenue.
  • Illustration 4 (dividend): Dividend declared 15 June 20X1 cannot be accrued in 20X0-X1 — right to receive arises on declaration, not proposal.
  • Illustration 5: Interest ₹10 lakh (time-proportion) and royalty ₹15 lakh (accrual per agreement) both recognised by X Ltd.
  • Example 7 (sale & repurchase): Goods "sold" for ₹5 lakh and repurchased at ₹6 lakh treated as a ₹5 lakh loan with ₹1 lakh interest — no sale.

Revenue ("top line") drives assessment of market share, growth prospects and business value, so a consistent standard for the amount and timing of recognition is critical for comparable, faithful financial statements — especially for start-ups that prioritise revenue over early profitability.

Chapter 9 Unit 1 — AS 12: Accounting for Government Grants

Source: 87717bos-aps2158-ch9u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 12 deals with accounting for government grants — subsidies, cash incentives, duty drawbacks, etc. — covering recognition, non-monetary grants, presentation (fixed-asset grants, revenue grants, promoters'-contribution grants), refunds and disclosures.
  • Grants are significant for two reasons: an appropriate method of accounting is needed, and it is desirable to indicate the extent to which the enterprise has benefited (aiding comparison across periods and enterprises).
  • Does not deal with: effects of changing prices/supplementary information, government assistance other than grants, or government participation in the ownership of the enterprise.

Key Definitions / Terms

Government grants
Assistance by government in cash or kind for past or future compliance with certain conditions; excludes assistance that cannot reasonably be valued and transactions indistinguishable from normal trading.
Capital approach
Grant treated as part of shareholders' funds.
Income approach
Grant taken to income over one or more periods.
Grants related to specific fixed assets
Grants whose primary condition is that the enterprise purchase, construct or acquire specified assets (may restrict type, location or holding period).
Promoters' contribution grant
Grant given with reference to total investment/capital outlay with no repayment ordinarily expected (e.g. central investment subsidy scheme).

How It Works / Logic

  • Recognition: not recognised until there is reasonable assurance that (a) the enterprise will comply with the attached conditions, and (b) the grant will be received. Receipt of a grant is not itself conclusive evidence that conditions have been or will be fulfilled.
  • Approach based on nature: grants with characteristics of promoters' contribution → capital approach (shareholders' funds); other grants → income approach.
  • Non-monetary grants: assets (e.g. land) given at concessional rates are recorded at acquisition cost; assets given free of cost are recorded at a nominal value.
  • Grants related to specific fixed assets — two acceptable methods: Method I — deduct the grant from the gross value of the asset; the grant is recognised in P&L over the asset's useful life via reduced depreciation; if the grant equals virtually the whole cost, show the asset at nominal value. Method II — treat the grant as deferred income, recognised in P&L systematically over the useful life; grants for non-depreciable assets are credited to capital reserve (unless obligations attach, in which case credited to income over the period the obligation cost is charged).
  • Grants related to revenue: presented as a credit in the P&L (separately or under a general heading like "Other Income"), or alternatively deducted from the related expense.
  • Promoters'-contribution nature: credited to capital reserve — neither distributable as dividend nor treated as deferred income.
  • Refund of grants (treated as an extraordinary item per AS 5): a revenue grant refund is applied first against any unamortised deferred credit, with the excess charged immediately to P&L; a fixed-asset grant refund is recorded by increasing the asset's book value (then depreciate the revised value prospectively over residual life) or by reducing the deferred income balance; a promoters'-contribution grant refund is reduced from capital reserve.

Examples

  • Illustration 1 vs 2: ₹50 lakh asset, ₹10 lakh grant, salvage ₹5 lakh, 5-year life — Method I depreciation ₹7,00,000 p.a. (grant netted from asset); Method II depreciation ₹9,00,000 p.a. with ₹2,00,000 grant taken to P&L annually.
  • Illustration 3 (Santosh): A ₹8 crore factory grant cannot be used to pay dividend (₹2 crore distribution improper); free land must be recorded at nominal value.
  • Illustration 5 (Top & Top): A 20%-of-investment subsidy with no repayment and no asset condition is a promoters'-contribution grant — credited to capital reserve, not revenue.
  • Illustration 8: On refund of ₹5 lakh (Method I), asset book value increased and depreciation revised prospectively to ₹3.67 lakh in year 2.
  • Illustration 9 (ABC Ltd.): ₹300 lakh grant refunded is added back to the WDV book value (₹614.40 → ₹914.40 lakh) and depreciated @20% prospectively over residual life.

Why It Matters / Application

Proper accounting shows the extent to which an enterprise has benefited from grants and ensures grants received for a specific purpose are used for that purpose, aiding comparison with prior periods and other enterprises. Disclosures cover the accounting policy adopted (including presentation methods) and the nature/extent of grants, including non-monetary grants at concessional rates or free of cost.

Chapter 9 Unit 2 — AS 14: Accounting for Amalgamations

Source: 87718bos-aps2158-ch9u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 14 (Revised) deals with accounting in the books of the transferee company for amalgamations and the treatment of any resulting goodwill or reserve.
  • An amalgamation is either in the nature of merger (accounted for under the pooling of interests method) or in the nature of purchase (accounted for under the purchase method).
  • Does not deal with acquisitions — the distinguishing feature of an acquisition is that the acquired company is not dissolved and its separate entity continues to exist.

Key Definitions / Terms

Amalgamation
Amalgamation pursuant to the Companies Act, 2013 (or other applicable statute); includes "merger".
Transferor / Transferee company
Company amalgamated into another / company into which the transferor is amalgamated.
Consideration
Aggregate of shares and other securities issued plus cash/other assets paid by the transferee to the shareholders of the transferor (payments to debenture holders are excluded).
Statutory reserves
Reserves (e.g. Development Allowance, Investment Allowance) created under a statute whose identity must be preserved for a specified period.
Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve
Separate line item (negative balance) created by a corresponding debit when statutory reserves are preserved under the purchase method; reversed with the reserve when the identity is no longer required.

How It Works / Logic

  • Merger — all five conditions must be met: (i) all assets and liabilities of the transferor become those of the transferee; (ii) shareholders holding ≥90% of the face value of equity shares of the transferor (excluding shares already held by the transferee/its subsidiaries) become equity shareholders of the transferee; (iii) consideration to those agreeing shareholders is discharged wholly by equity shares (cash only for fractional shares); (iv) the transferor's business is intended to continue; (v) no adjustment to book values except to ensure uniform accounting policies. Failing any one → amalgamation in the nature of purchase.
  • Pooling of interests method (merger): assets, liabilities and reserves of the transferor are recorded at their existing carrying amounts; only minimal changes are made; conflicting accounting policies are aligned to a uniform set (effects reported per AS 5).
  • Purchase method: assets and liabilities are incorporated at existing carrying amounts, or the consideration is allocated to individual identifiable assets and liabilities at their fair values at the date of amalgamation (may include items not recorded by the transferor).
  • Consideration is computed by the Net Payment Method first (payments/shares given to equity and preference shareholders); if it cannot be computed that way, the Net Assets Method applies (assets less liabilities — at book value for a merger, at agreed value for a purchase). Contingent additional payments are included when probable and reasonably estimable; otherwise recognised when determinable.
  • Reserves of transferor — merger: identity is preserved (General Reserve stays General Reserve, Capital Reserve stays Capital Reserve, etc.); reserves distributable as dividend before amalgamation remain so after. Reserves are adjusted for uniform policies and for the difference between share capital issued (plus any cash/other consideration) and the transferor's share capital.
  • Reserves — purchase: identity is not preserved except for statutory reserves. Consideration is deducted from the value of net assets acquired: a negative result (consideration > net assets) is debited to goodwill; a positive result is credited to capital reserve. Statutory reserves are preserved via a corresponding debit to Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve (shown as a separate line item, not set off against the statutory reserve).
  • Goodwill on amalgamation is a payment in anticipation of future income, treated as an asset amortised systematically over its useful life — generally a period not exceeding five years unless a longer period is justified. Factors: foreseeable life of the business/industry, product obsolescence and demand changes, service life of key employees, competitor actions, legal/regulatory/contractual provisions.
  • Balance of P&L account: in a merger it is aggregated with the transferee's balance (or transferred to General Reserve); in a purchase it loses its identity (whether debit or credit).
  • Amalgamation after the balance sheet date (but before issuance of statements): disclosed per AS 4 but not incorporated in the statements; may support the going concern assumption.

Examples

  • Illustration 1: Equity ₹4,20,000 + 15% preference ₹1,70,000 + ₹17,000 redemption premium = purchase consideration ₹6,07,000; debenture-holder payments excluded.
  • Illustration 2 (C Ltd.): Purchase-method amalgamation of A Ltd. and B Ltd.; goodwill ₹110 lakh (A) and capital reserve ₹90 lakh (B); Investment Allowance (statutory) reserve of ₹100 lakh preserved with an Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve of (₹100 lakh); securities premium ₹1,650 lakh.
  • Scenario Q9: Under pooling, excess of ₹5,00,000 (₹55,00,000 consideration over ₹50,00,000 share capital) adjusted through General Reserve.
  • Scenario Q10 (Tina–Rina): Equity ₹7,50,000 (50,000 × ₹15) + cash ₹50,000 + 12% preference ₹2,00,000 = purchase consideration ₹10,00,000; securities premium ₹2,50,000; debentures excluded.

Chapter 10 Unit 1 — AS 21: Consolidated Financial Statements

Source: 87719bos-aps2158-ch10u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Purpose of CFS. A holding (parent) company and its subsidiaries are legally distinct but in substance operate as a single economic entity (a group). Consolidated Financial Statements (CFS) present the group as one enterprise so users see the economic resources controlled, the obligations owed, and the results achieved. The parent still prepares its own standalone statements (mandatory in India under the Companies Act, 2013 and Income Tax Act, 1961); CFS are prepared in addition.
  • AS 21 scope. Effective from 1-4-2001, AS 21 lays down principles and procedures for preparing CFS. It does not mandate that an enterprise prepare CFS, but if CFS are presented (to comply with a statute or otherwise) they must follow AS 21. In the parent's separate statements, investment in a subsidiary is accounted under AS 13. AS 21 excludes amalgamation accounting (AS 14), associates (AS 23) and joint ventures (AS 27).
  • Components of CFS. Consolidated Balance Sheet; Consolidated Statement of Profit & Loss; Notes/explanatory schedules; and a Consolidated Cash Flow Statement (only if the parent presents its own cash flow statement). Format follows the parent's own statements per Schedule III. There are no "group books of accounts" — CFS are built from each entity's standalone statements plus consolidation adjustments.
  • Companies Act linkage. A company with subsidiaries/associates/JVs must prepare CFS per Schedule III and applicable AS, laid at the AGM, with Form AOC-1 giving salient features of subsidiaries.

Key Definitions / Terms

Parent / Holding company
An enterprise having one or more subsidiaries [Sec 2(46)].
Subsidiary
An enterprise controlled by the parent [Sec 2(87): parent controls Board composition, or holds/controls more than one-half of total share capital, directly or via other subsidiaries]. Sec 19 bars a subsidiary from holding shares in its holding company (limited exceptions: as legal representative, trustee, or a pre-existing member).
Control
(a) Ownership, directly or indirectly, of more than one-half of voting power; OR (b) control of the composition of the Board/governing body so as to obtain economic benefits. Control over gratuity/PF trusts (no economic benefit) is ignored.
Group
A parent and all its subsidiaries.
Minority Interest (MI)
That part of net results and net assets of a subsidiary attributable to interests not owned by the parent. Shown within equity but separately from the parent's equity.
Equity
Residual interest in assets after deducting all liabilities (i.e. net worth = share capital + reserves).
Wholly vs partly owned
Wholly owned = 100% held (no MI); partly owned = more than 50% but under 100% (MI exists).
Cost of control
Goodwill / Capital reserve arising when cost of investment differs from the parent's share of the subsidiary's equity at acquisition.

How It Works / Logic

  • Exclusion from consolidation. A subsidiary is excluded when (a) control is intended to be temporary — acquired and held exclusively for disposal in the "near future" (ordinarily <12 months); or (b) it operates under severe long-term restrictions impairing fund transfer to the parent. Excluded investments are accounted under AS 13; reasons must be disclosed. Dissimilar business activities are not a valid ground for exclusion. LLPs/partnership firms that are subsidiaries (or associates/JVs) must be consolidated.
  • Consolidation procedure. Combine the parent's and subsidiaries' items line-by-line (cash + cash, receivables + receivables, etc.), then adjust: (1) eliminate the parent's cost of investment against its share of the subsidiary's equity at acquisition date, recognising goodwill or capital reserve; (2) eliminate intra-group balances, transactions (sales, expenses, dividends) and unrealised profits in full; (3) identify minority interest in net income and in net assets, presenting each separately; (4) include a subsidiary's results from the date the relationship begins until it ceases.
  • Cost of control (goodwill / capital reserve). Goodwill = Cost of investment − Parent's share of subsidiary's equity at acquisition. If negative, the excess is Capital Reserve. Only the pre-acquisition equity (share capital + pre-acquisition/capital reserves and profits) is set against cost.
  • Pre- vs post-acquisition. Subsidiary reserves/profits up to the acquisition date are capital (pre-acquisition) profits — the parent's share is absorbed into the cost-of-control computation. Profits after acquisition are revenue (post-acquisition) — the parent's share is added to consolidated reserves/P&L. If shares are acquired mid-year, the year's profit is split (time basis, absent specific information) into pre- and post-acquisition portions.
  • Minority interest computation. MI = MI share of paid-up capital + MI share of all reserves/profits (pre- and post-acquisition) at the reporting date + MI share of preference capital held outside. If losses attributable to MI exceed its equity, the excess is borne by the majority (unless MI has a binding obligation); later profits go to the majority until recovered.
  • Revaluation of subsidiary assets. Done only for consolidation (not in the subsidiary's own books). Revaluation profit/loss at acquisition is capital; a fall after acquisition is revenue. Additional depreciation on upward revaluation (or reversal on downward) is charged to post-acquisition profit and split between holding and MI.
  • Dividend from subsidiary. Dividend out of pre-acquisition profits is a capital receipt → credited to Investment A/c (reduces cost). Dividend out of post-acquisition profits is income → credited to P&L. Mid-year acquisitions require time-apportionment. Bonus shares from the subsidiary: no entry, only share count rises (though bonus out of pre-acquisition vs post-acquisition profits changes the capital/revenue split).
  • Intra-group eliminations. CFS must show only external transactions. Intra-group balances (mutual debtors/creditors, loans, bills) are set off; intra-group sales are removed from both turnover and cost of sales. Unrealised profit in closing inventory (goods still held within the group) and in intra-group fixed-asset transfers is eliminated in full, reducing the asset to cost-to-group. Downstream (parent sells to subsidiary): whole unrealised profit hits consolidated P&L. Upstream (subsidiary sells to parent): unrealised profit is split between holding and MI per shareholding. Unrealised losses are eliminated unless cost cannot be recovered (NRV test).
  • Uniform policies & reporting dates. CFS use uniform accounting policies for like transactions; a member using different policies (e.g. FIFO vs weighted average) is restated before consolidation, or the fact/proportions disclosed if impracticable. Statements should share the same reporting date; differences must not exceed six months, with adjustment for significant events between dates, and be consistent period to period.
  • Preference capital & disposal. Cumulative preference shares of a subsidiary held outside the group are treated like a liability — the parent computes its profit share after deducting cumulative preference dividends (whether declared or not); non-cumulative only when received. On disposal of a subsidiary, profit/loss = proceeds − carrying amount of net assets at disposal date, recognised in consolidated P&L; thereafter the residual investment follows AS 13 (disposal is excluded at Intermediate level).

Examples

  • Goodwill vs capital reserve (Examples 1–3): P Ltd. buys 100% of S Ltd. (net worth ₹1,000) — paying ₹1,000 gives nil goodwill; paying ₹1,200 gives ₹200 goodwill; paying less would give capital reserve. The break-up of net worth (capital vs reserves) is irrelevant to the goodwill figure.
  • Minority interest (Illustration 1): MI at any date = MI% × (share capital + P&L balance). Consolidated P&L = holding's own P&L + holding's share of post-acquisition profit.
  • Losses exceeding MI (Illustration 4): 30% MI; once MI equity is exhausted, further losses are borne by the holding company, later recovered from MI's share of subsequent profits.
  • Pre-acquisition dividend (Illustrations 3, 6): Dividend declared on/for the pre-acquisition period is deducted from cost of investment before computing goodwill/capital reserve.
  • Mid-year acquisition (Illustrations 7, 11): Time-apportion the year's profit; the pre-acquisition share loads cost of control, the post-acquisition share adds to consolidated reserves.
  • Bonus & uniform policy (Illustrations 9, 16): bonus from post-acquisition reserves shifts amounts between goodwill and reserves; FIFO/weighted-average and provision differences are restated before consolidating.

Chapter 10 Unit 2 — AS 23: Accounting for Investments in Associates in Consolidated Financial Statements

Source: 87720bos-aps2158-ch10u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 23 (effective for accounting periods on/after 1-4-2002) lays down the principles and procedures for recognising investments in associates in the consolidated financial statements (CFS) of the investor, and their effect on the group's financial operations.
  • An associate is one in which the investor has significant influence, but which is neither a subsidiary nor a joint venture.
  • In CFS, investments in associates are accounted for using the equity method. In the investor's separate financial statements, AS 13 (Accounting for Investments) applies instead.
  • Reference to AS 23 is compulsory for companies following AS 21 and preparing CFS for their group.
  • Scope link: existence of significant influence (not joint control, not control) is what triggers AS 23.

Key Definitions / Terms

Subsidiary
An enterprise that is controlled by another enterprise (the parent).
Parent
An enterprise that has one or more subsidiaries.
Group
A parent and all its subsidiaries.
Equity method
A method whereby the investment is initially recorded at cost, identifying any goodwill/capital reserve at acquisition. The carrying amount is thereafter adjusted for the post-acquisition change in the investor's share of net assets of the investee. The consolidated statement of profit and loss reflects the investor's share of the investee's results.
Equity
The residual interest in the assets of an enterprise after deducting all its liabilities.
Consolidated financial statements
Financial statements of a group presented as those of a single enterprise.
Associate
An enterprise in which the investor has significant influence and which is neither a subsidiary nor a joint venture of the investor.
Significant influence
The power to participate in the financial and/or operating policy decisions of the investee but not control over those policies. Presumed to exist when the investor holds, directly or indirectly through subsidiaries, 20% or more of the voting power (unless clearly demonstrated otherwise); presumed absent below 20% (unless clearly demonstrated). May be gained by share ownership, statute or agreement.
Control
Either (a) ownership, directly or indirectly through subsidiary(ies), of more than one-half of the voting power; or (b) control of the composition of the board of directors / governing body so as to obtain economic benefits from the entity's activities. (Controlling a gratuity/PF trust's governing body is excluded — objective is not economic benefit.)

How It Works / Logic

  • Indicators of significant influence: voting power; representation on the board/governing body; participation in policy-making; material inter-company transactions (sale of goods/services, technical know-how); interchange of managerial personnel; provision of essential technical information.
  • Substance over 20% rule: A 20%+ holder can be shown NOT to have significant influence (then not an associate); a <20% holder can be shown to have it (via the indicators). Majority ownership by another investor does not preclude significant influence.
  • Deemed power to appoint a director/member exists if: (i) a person cannot be appointed without that enterprise exercising such power; or (ii) appointment follows necessarily from a position held in that enterprise; or (iii) the director/member is nominated by that enterprise or its subsidiary.
  • Equity method — step-by-step (broad conclusions): (a) in CFS the investment is recorded at cost; (b) any surplus/deficit between cost and share of net assets is recorded as capital reserve/goodwill; (c) distributions (dividends) received reduce the carrying amount; (d) any subsequent change in share of net assets adjusts cost of investment and goodwill/capital reserve; (e) Consolidated P&L shows the investor's share in the investee's results.
  • Goodwill vs capital reserve: If cost of investment > share of equity → Goodwill. If share of equity > cost → Capital Reserve. Goodwill/capital reserve is included in the carrying amount of the investment but disclosed separately.
  • Carrying amount roll-forward format:
    Cost of Investment (Share of Net Assets + Goodwill / − Capital Reserve)xxx
    Add: Investor's share of post-acquisition profitxxx
    Less: Investor's share of dividend received(xxx)
    Less: Investor's share of post-acquisition loss(xxx)
    Carrying Amount of Investmentxxx
  • Step acquisition: The 20%–50% stake may be acquired in one go or over several transactions, and may be increased (e.g. 25% → 30%); goodwill/capital reserve is computed and adjusted at each transaction (pre-acquisition profits treated as capital, post-acquisition as revenue).
  • Losses exceeding carrying amount: When the investor's share of losses equals/exceeds the carrying amount, recognition of further losses ordinarily stops and the investment is reported at nil. Additional losses are provided only to the extent the investor has incurred obligations/made payments on the associate's behalf (guarantees/commitments). If the associate later reports profits, the investor resumes its share only after profits equal the previously unrecognised losses.
  • Reporting date & policies: Reporting dates should ideally be the same; if different, adjust for significant transactions/events between the dates, and the difference must not exceed six months. Uniform accounting policies must be used for like transactions; otherwise adjust the individual statements.
  • Impairment: The carrying amount is reduced to recognise a decline, other than temporary, in value — determined and made for each investment individually.

Circumstances / Discontinuing the equity method

  • Equity method NOT used when: (a) control/investment is intended to be temporary (acquired and held exclusively with a view to disposal in the near future); or (b) the associate operates under severe long-term restrictions significantly impairing its ability to transfer funds to the investor. In both cases account under AS 13.
  • Discontinue the equity method from the date the investor: (a) ceases to have significant influence but retains its investment (in whole/part); or (b) the method is no longer appropriate due to severe long-term restrictions. Thereafter account under AS 13, treating the carrying amount at that date as cost. Reasons for not applying the equity method must be disclosed in CFS.

Disclosure

  • Appropriate listing and description of associates, including proportion of ownership interest and, if different, proportion of voting power.
  • Investments accounted under equity method classified as long-term investments and disclosed separately in the consolidated balance sheet; investor's share of profits/losses disclosed separately in the consolidated P&L; share of any extraordinary or prior period items disclosed separately.
  • Name(s) of associates with different reporting date(s) and the difference in dates.
  • If an associate uses different accounting policies and adjustment is impracticable — disclose the fact with a brief description of the differences.
  • Reasons if an associate is not accounted for using the equity method.
  • Goodwill/capital reserve on acquisition disclosed separately though included in carrying amount.
  • Contingencies (AS 4): disclose in CFS the investor's share of contingencies and capital commitments of an associate for which it is also contingently liable, and contingencies arising because the investor is severally liable for the associate's liabilities.

Why It Matters / Application

A 20%+ (but <50%) stake signals the investor is not merely a passive dividend-seeker but participates in decision-making and bears a measure of responsibility for the associate's performance. Recognising income only on dividends received would understate the true return, because distributions may bear little relation to the associate's actual performance. The equity method extends the CFS to include the investor's share of the associate's results, giving a more informative picture of the group's net assets and net income and enabling more useful ratio analysis (stewardship reporting).

Note

For consolidation, holdings are computed on a look-through basis: e.g. A holds 90% of B and 10% of C directly, B holds 11% of C — A's indirect CFS holding in C is 10% + (90% × 11%) = 19.9%, yet A's total direct+indirect holding of 21% still makes C an associate of A. Separate FS use AS 13; CFS use AS 23. Pre-acquisition dividend reduces the cost of investment (not income).

Chapter 10 Unit 3 — AS 27: Financial Reporting of Interests in Joint Ventures

Source: 87721bos-aps2158-ch10u3.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 27 (effective for accounting periods on/after 1-4-2002) sets out principles and procedures for accounting for interests in joint ventures and for reporting joint venture assets, liabilities, income and expenses in the financial statements of venturers and investors, regardless of the structure/form of the JV activities.
  • It recognises three broad types: Jointly Controlled Operations (JCO), Jointly Controlled Assets (JCA) and Jointly Controlled Entities (JCE).
  • The proportionate consolidation method for JVs in CFS applies only when the venturer prepares CFS.
  • The existence of a contractual arrangement establishing joint control distinguishes a JV interest from an investment in an associate (significant influence, AS 23).
  • An investor in a JV that does not have joint control reports its interest per AS 13, AS 21 and AS 23 as appropriate. Popular examples: HUL, Tata Starbucks, Tata SIA (Vistara).

Key Definitions / Terms

Joint venture
A contractual arrangement whereby two or more parties undertake an economic activity that is subject to joint control.
Joint control
The contractually agreed sharing of control over an economic activity.
Control
The power to govern the financial and operating policies of an economic activity so as to obtain benefits from it.
Venturer
A party to a JV that has joint control over that JV.
Investor in a JV
A party to a JV that does not have joint control over it.
Proportionate consolidation
A method of accounting and reporting whereby a venturer's share of each of the assets, liabilities, income and expenses of a jointly controlled entity is reported as separate line items in the venturer's financial statements.

How It Works / Logic

  • Essential conditions for a JV: (a) two or more parties come together (individual, BOI, AOP, company, firm); (b) they undertake an economic activity (profit-motive); (c) they have joint control over it (share operating/financial decisions and results); (d) there exists a contractual agreement (written contract, minutes of a venturers' meeting, articles/by-laws, or any other written form).
  • Contractual arrangement content (ideal): the activity, duration and reporting obligations; appointment of the board/governing body and voting rights; capital contributions; sharing of output, income, expenses or results. Its object is to distribute economic control so that no venturer has unilateral control; it identifies decisions requiring consent of all venturers vs a specified majority.
  • An agreement signed merely to safeguard a lender's right does not make that party a venturer (e.g. IDBI's protective agreement over an L&T–Tantia JV entity). Naming one venturer as operator/manager does not disqualify it as venturer nor give it control — the operator acts within policies agreed by the venturers.
  • A JV with a contractual agreement is excluded from AS 27 only if the investment qualifies as a subsidiary under AS 21 (then AS 21 applies).
  • JCO — Jointly Controlled Operations: No separate entity; venturers use their own resources/assets/employees, raise their own funds, incur own expenses, realise own sales. No separate books for the JV (only internal management reporting, if any). A venturer recognises in its own (and hence consolidated) FS: (a) the assets it controls and liabilities it incurs; and (b) the expenses it incurs and its share of the income earned. Because everything is already in the venturer's own FS, no consolidation adjustment is needed.
  • JCA — Jointly Controlled Assets: No separate entity; venturers jointly own assets used to generate economic benefit for each. Each venturer shows in its FS: its share of the joint assets (classified by nature, not as an investment), its share of jointly incurred expenses/liabilities per contract, plus its own expenses and income. Already recognised in separate FS, so no consolidation adjustment needed.
  • JCO vs JCA: JCO — venturers use their own assets; JCA — assets are jointly owned. JCO shares all expenses and revenues at agreed ratios; JCA shares only expenses on the joint assets at the agreed ratio.
  • JCE — Jointly Controlled Entities: A new corporation/partnership/other entity is created; it owns its assets, incurs its own liabilities/expenses and makes its own sales; net result shared per contract. Each venturer records its contribution as an investment (AS 13) in its separate FS; the JCE keeps its own books. In CFS the venturer uses proportionate consolidation.
  • Proportionate consolidation features: (a) substance over form; (b) venturer's share of joint assets, liabilities, expenses and income shown on separate lines; (c) most provisions mirror AS 21; (d) reporting dates ideally same, else adjust for significant events, difference not to exceed six months; (e) uniform accounting policies (else adjust); (f) an asset/liability is not offset against another, nor income against expense, unless legally permitted and it settles an obligation/writes off an asset; (g) at acquisition, cost of investment > share of net assets → goodwill; share of net assets > cost → capital reserve (use carrying amount if it differs from cost); (h) an investor without joint control is treated like an associate (AS 23) — excess losses over interest recognised, resumed profits offset unrecognised losses first.
  • Discontinuing proportionate consolidation: stop from the date the venturer (a) ceases to have joint control but retains its investment; or (b) severe long-term restrictions make it inappropriate. Thereafter account by interest: >50% → AS 21; 20%–50% → AS 23; all other cases → AS 13. Carrying amount at cessation date is regarded as cost thereafter.
  • Not used at all when: (a) the investment is intended to be temporary (held exclusively for disposal in the near future); or (b) severe long-term restrictions impair fund transfer. Then account under AS 13.

Transactions between a venturer and the JV

  • Venturer sells/contributes assets to the JV (downstream): recognise only the portion of gain/loss attributable to the other venturers. Recognise the full loss immediately only when the contribution/sale evidences a reduction in net realisable value of current assets or an impairment loss.
  • Venturer buys assets from the JV (upstream): do not recognise its share of the JV's profit on the transaction until it resells the asset to a third party. Losses treated like profits, except recognised in full immediately when they represent an NRV reduction/impairment.
  • For a JCE: the above eliminations apply only in the CFS; in the venturer's own financial statements the profit/loss from such transactions is recognised in full.

  • Proportionate consolidation (Illustration 4): A & B form C Ltd (50:50). A's balance sheet adds A's own figures + 50% of C Ltd's (e.g. Reserves ₹18,00,000 + ₹6,00,000 = ₹24,00,000; PPE ₹30,50,000 + ₹9,75,000 = ₹40,25,000), Investment in JV eliminated, total ₹42,50,000.
  • Downstream gain example: A & B (50:50) form entity J; A contributes property (FV ₹110 cr, carrying ₹100 cr), B contributes equipment (FV ₹120 cr, carrying ₹80 cr). A's gain in CFS = A's share of B's FV contributed (50%×120 = 60) − A's share of own asset's carrying value (50%×100 = 50) = ₹10 cr.
  • Diminution scenario: Long-term JV investment (interest in a JCE) is carried at cost per AS 13; a decline is written down only if it is other than temporary. Continuous losses alone don't force a write-down if futuristic/profitable plans with reliable presumptions (tenders, strong order book) make the decline temporary.

Reporting by an investor / Operators / Disclosure

  • Investor without joint control: recognises its share of results and its investment per AS 13 (separate FS); in CFS per AS 13, AS 21 or AS 23 as appropriate.
  • Operators of JVs: payment to an operator is recognised as an expense in CFS and, in the operator's books, as revenue per AS 9. Management fees received by a venturer-operator are treated likewise.
  • Contingent liabilities (unless loss remote), disclosed in aggregate separately: (a) those the venturer incurred re its JV interests and its share of those incurred jointly; (b) its share of the JVs' own contingent liabilities for which it is contingently liable; (c) those arising because the venturer is contingently liable for other venturers' liabilities.
  • Commitments re JV interests disclosed in aggregate separately: (a) capital commitments of the venturer re its JV interests and its share of jointly incurred capital commitments; (b) its share of the JVs' own capital commitments.
  • Disclose a list of all JVs and description of interests in significant JVs; for JCEs also the proportion of ownership interest, name and country of incorporation/residence; and, in separate FS, the aggregate amounts of assets, liabilities, income and expenses relating to interests in JCEs.

Why It Matters / Application

Entities enter JVs to share risk and expense and pool know-how/skills. The correct accounting depends entirely on the contractual structure: JCO and JCA require no separate consolidation because the venturer already records its own assets, liabilities, income and expenses; only a JCE requires proportionate consolidation in the CFS. Applying substance over form, proportionate consolidation brings transparency by showing the venturer's real share of the venture's resources and results line by line, rather than a single investment figure.

Note

Multi-tier example: SEAS Ltd (parent) holds 9% and its subsidiary ADI Ltd holds 23% in associate ASOC Ltd. ADI equity-accounts its 23%; SEAS consolidates ADI (carrying ASOC's 23% automatically) and separately equity-accounts its own 9% in the CFS — because at the group level the combined holding gives significant influence. Fair value is not used as deemed cost on cessation — the carrying amount at the date joint control ceases is regarded as cost thereafter.

Chapter 11 Unit 1 — Preparation of Financial Statements of Companies

Source: 87723bos-aps2158-ch11u1.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Meaning of company [Sec 2(20)]: a company incorporated under the Companies Act, 2013 or any previous company law. Types include company limited by guarantee [2(21)], limited by shares [2(22)], foreign company [2(42)], Government company [2(45)] (≥51% paid-up capital held by Central/State Govt.), One Person Company [2(62)], private company [2(68)], public company [2(71)], small company [2(85)], unlimited company [2(92)], holding [2(46)] and subsidiary company [2(87)].
  • Maintenance of books (Sec 128): every company must keep at its registered office books of account and financial statements for every financial year, on accrual basis under the double-entry system, giving a true and fair view. The Registrar must be informed within 7 days if the Board decides to keep books elsewhere; books plus vouchers of at least 8 preceding financial years must be preserved. Books may be kept in electronic mode with a tamper-proof audit trail feature (mandatory from 1 April 2022) and a daily backup on servers in India.
  • Statutory books required under various sections: Register of Investments (187), Charges (85), Members (88), Debenture-holders (88), Minute Books (118), Contracts in which directors are interested (189), Directors & KMP and their shareholding (170), Loans & Investments (186), plus statistical/share registers (application & allotment, call, certificate, transfer, dividend registers).
  • Annual return (Sec 92): filed with the Registrar within 60 days of the AGM (or the date it should have been held).
  • Financial statements [Sec 2(40)]: Balance Sheet, Statement of Profit and Loss (or income & expenditure account for non-profit), Cash Flow Statement, Statement of Changes in Equity (if applicable) and explanatory notes. OPC, small, dormant and start-up private companies may omit the cash flow statement.
  • Financial statements must give a true and fair view (Sec 129), comply with Accounting Standards (Sec 133) and follow the form of Schedule III — Balance Sheet in Part I and Statement of Profit and Loss in Part II.
  • Divisible/distributable profit — not all book profit is available for dividend; availability depends on composition, priority provisions and appropriations.

Key Definitions / Terms

Private company [2(68)]
Restricts transfer of shares, limits members to 200 (except OPC), and prohibits public invitation to subscribe.
Public company [2(71)]
Not a private company and has prescribed minimum paid-up capital; a subsidiary of a public company is deemed public.
Small company [2(85)]
Non-public company with paid-up capital not exceeding the prescribed limit (₹4 crores per amended Rules) and turnover not exceeding the prescribed limit (₹40 crores); excludes holding/subsidiary, Sec 8 and special-Act companies.
SMC (Small and Medium Sized Company)
Per Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021: securities not listed/being listed; not a bank/FI/insurer; turnover (excluding other income) ≤ ₹250 crores; borrowings ≤ ₹50 crores; and not a holding/subsidiary of a non-SMC.
Subsidiary [2(87)]
Company in which the holding company controls the Board composition or exercises/controls more than half the total share capital.
Dividend [Sec 2(35)]
Distribution of divisible profit among members in proportion to shares held; includes interim dividend.
Divisible/Distributable profit
Profit legally available for distribution after providing depreciation (current and past arrears) and mandatory appropriations, and excluding capital profits.
Reserve
Portion of earnings/surplus (capital or revenue) appropriated by management for a general or specific purpose — distinct from a provision, which per AS 29 is "a liability which can be measured only by using a substantial degree of estimation".
Free reserves
Reserves available for distribution as dividend; dividend cannot be declared out of reserves other than free reserves.

  • Applicability: Insurance, banking and electricity companies (and any class with a prescribed form) follow their governing statute; all other companies use Schedule III. Division I = companies applying Accounting Standards (Sec 133); Division II = companies applying Ind AS; Division III = NBFCs applying Ind AS.

  • Presentation rules: debit balance of the Statement of P&L (after all allocations and appropriations) is shown as a negative figure under "Surplus"; the balance of Reserves & Surplus is shown net even if the aggregate is negative. Current maturities of long-term loans are shown under short-term borrowings (MCA notification, 24 March 2021).
  • Trade receivables disclosure (Division I): classified into long-term (non-current) and short-term (current), sub-classified as secured-considered good, unsecured-considered good, and doubtful; allowance for bad & doubtful debts disclosed separately; dues from directors/officers/related firms stated separately; and an ageing schedule (<6 months, 6 months–1 year, 1–2, 2–3, >3 years) split into undisputed/disputed × good/doubtful.
  • Dividend (Sec 123(1)): declared only out of (a) current-year profits after depreciation per Sec 123(2), or (b) undistributed past profits after depreciation, or (c) both, or (d) money provided by Government under a guarantee. No dividend out of reserves other than free reserves; capital cannot be returned as dividend.
  • Depreciation (Sec 123(2)): must be provided to the extent specified in Schedule II, which prescribes depreciation on the basis of the useful life of assets (rather than fixed rates); on sale/discard/demolition/destruction, the excess of WDV over scrap sale proceeds is written off in the same year.
  • Dividend out of reserves (inadequate/absent profits) — Companies (Declaration & Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014: (1) rate ≤ average of last 3 years (not applicable if no dividend declared in each of those 3 years); (2) total drawn ≤ 1/10 (one-tenth) of paid-up capital + free reserves per latest audited statement; (3) amount drawn first sets off current-year losses; (4) balance of reserves after withdrawal must not fall below 15% of paid-up capital.
  • Interim dividend (Sec 123): the Board may declare it out of surplus in P&L and current-year profits; if a loss is incurred up to the preceding quarter, the interim rate must not exceed the average of the preceding three years.
  • Deposit & payment: declared dividend must be deposited in a separate scheduled-bank account within 5 days; paid only to the registered shareholder, only in cash (cheque/warrant/electronic mode); calls-in-advance do not rank for dividend; partly paid shares may be paid dividend in proportion to paid-up amount (Sec 51) if the articles permit; preference shareholders receive their fixed rate first, subject to profits and Board recommendation.
  • Unpaid dividend (Sec 124): unpaid/unclaimed within 30 days of declaration → transfer to a special "Unpaid Dividend Account" within 7 days of the 30-day expiry; a statement of names is placed on the website within 90 days; default attracts interest at 12% p.a. Amounts unclaimed for 7 years (with interest) → transferred to the Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF) (Sec 125), and the related shares are also transferred to IEPF. Non-compliance: company fine ₹5–25 lakh; officer in default ₹1–5 lakh.
  • Transfer to reserves: the Board may freely appropriate part of the profit to reserves (Sec 123(1)); some transfers are mandated by law (e.g. Banking Regulation Act) or by loan covenants for a sinking/repayment fund.
  • Managerial remuneration (basics): managerial remuneration is computed with reference to net profits calculated in the manner laid down by the Companies Act and is subject to the overall ceiling prescribed under the Act for directors/managers.

Chapter 11 Unit 2 — Cash Flow Statements

Source: 87724bos-aps2158-ch11u2.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements" provides information on the historical changes in cash and cash equivalents by classifying flows during the period into Operating, Investing and Financing activities.
  • It helps users assess an enterprise's ability to generate cash and cash equivalents, and the timing/certainty of that generation — a basis for economic decisions, and a measure of liquidity, quality of earnings and solvency.
  • Benefits: shows changes in cash & cash equivalents; identifies cash generated from trading operations; reveals the operating surplus available for investment; shows the split between cash used for dividend/tax and cash ploughed back; and is a useful planning tool.
  • Sources of cash: issue of shares/debentures and long-term loans, sale of investments and fixed assets, cash from operations. Applications of cash: redemption of shares/debentures and loan repayment, purchase of investments/fixed assets, payment of tax and dividend.
  • Cash from operating activities may be computed by the direct method or the indirect method.

Key Definitions / Terms

Cash
Cash in hand and demand deposits with banks.
Cash equivalents
Short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible into known amounts of cash with insignificant risk of value change — typically maturity of about 3 months or less from acquisition. Purpose: to deploy idle cash for short periods to meet short-term commitments (e.g. preference shares acquired shortly before redemption, short-maturity bank deposits).
Operating activities
Principal revenue-generating activities; the amount of operating cash flow is a key indicator of whether operations generate enough cash to maintain operating capability, pay dividends, repay loans and make new investments without external financing.
Investing activities
Acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents; separate disclosure shows expenditure made for resources intended to generate future income and cash flows.
Financing activities
Activities that change the size and composition of the owner's capital (including preference share capital) and borrowings; separate disclosure helps predict claims on future cash flows by providers of funds.

How It Works / Logic

  • Operating examples: cash from sale of goods/services, royalties/fees/commissions; cash paid to suppliers and to/on behalf of employees; insurance premiums/claims; income-tax payments/refunds (unless identifiable with financing/investing); and, for financial institutions, advances/loans and dealing/trading securities.
  • Investing examples: payments to acquire fixed assets (incl. capitalised R&D and self-constructed assets) and to acquire equity/debt of other entities; receipts from sale of PPE, intangibles and investments; advances/loans made to and repaid by other parties; futures/forward/option/swap contract flows (unless held for trading or classed as financing).
  • Financing examples: proceeds from issuing shares/other equity; payments to owners to redeem shares; proceeds from debentures/loans/notes/bonds/mortgages; repayment of amounts borrowed; and lease-liability principal payments under a finance lease.
  • Direct method: reports major classes of gross cash receipts (from customers, receivables, trade commission) and gross cash payments (to suppliers, employees, overheads, taxes). Amounts are obtained from records or by adjusting sales/cost of sales for changes in inventories and operating receivables/payables, and by removing non-cash items and investing/financing items. It gives a break-up useful for forecasting and is considered more appropriate.
  • Indirect method: starts with net profit (before tax) and adjusts for (a) working-capital changes in inventories and operating receivables/payables, (b) non-cash items such as depreciation, goodwill/preliminary expenses written off, and (c) items whose cash effects are financing/investing (interest, dividend, gain/loss on sale of assets). It is more popular in practice but fails to give the break-up of cash from operations.
  • Working-capital rule (both methods): ADD decrease in current assets and increase in current liabilities; DEDUCT increase in current assets and decrease in current liabilities.
  • Indirect-method proforma: Net profit + non-cash/non-operating expenses (depreciation, loss on sale, provision for tax) − non-cash/non-operating incomes (profit on sale) = adjusted profit; then apply working-capital changes to reach cash from operations; then deduct income taxes paid.
  • Interest, dividend and tax: shown separately. Interest/dividend paid and received relating to financing or investing are shown under those heads; income taxes are operating unless specifically identifiable with financing/investing.
  • Tax paid working: tax expense + opening tax liability − closing tax liability = income tax paid. Dividend/interest paid similarly reconciled through opening/closing payable balances.

Examples

  • Intelligent Ltd. classification: loans to suppliers/employees = operating; loans to and investment in subsidiaries (and dividend received) = investing; dividend paid = financing outflow; TDS on investment interest = investing outflow; TDS on supplier-advance interest = operating outflow; insurance claim for a fixed asset lost by fire = extraordinary item shown under investing.
  • Ajay Ltd. (extract): goodwill amortisation ₹25,000 and debenture interest ₹75,000 added back to operating; debenture proceeds ₹2,50,000 and interest paid (net of unpaid ₹5,000) ₹70,000 under financing — interest computed on the closing debenture balance.
  • Hills Ltd. / X Ltd. (direct): from a summary cash account — receipts from customers less payments to suppliers/employees/overheads = cash generated from operations; less taxes = net operating cash; investing = fixed-asset purchase/sale; financing = share issue, loan repayment, dividend.
  • Ryan Ltd. (indirect): net profit before tax ₹23,000, add depreciation ₹37,000, interest expense ₹23,000, loss on plant ₹3,000, less gain on investments ₹12,000 and interest income ₹6,000 → operating profit before WC changes ₹68,000 → net operating cash ₹47,000; issue of ₹1,00,000 bonds for plant shown as a non-cash note.
  • Gamma Ltd. (direct): conversion of debentures to equity (₹15 crores) and plant taken in part-exchange (book value ₹12 crores transferred at ₹10 crores, ₹11 crores paid) disclosed as significant non-cash items.
  • ABC Ltd.: operating cash of ₹445 lakhs derived identically by both direct method (receipts less payments) and indirect method (profit before tax adjusted for depreciation, interest, tax and working-capital changes).
  • MCQ pointers: conversion of debt to equity = non-cash (not shown); purchase of another company's bonds/shares by a non-dealer = investing; dividend paid and income-tax payments are not investing.

Why It Matters / Application

Separate disclosure of the three activities helps users predict future cash flows and the claims on cash by capital and loan providers, and distinguishes cash generated by trading from cash raised or repaid through financing/investing. Conversion of debt to equity and part-exchange of assets are non-cash items — excluded from the statement but disclosed by way of note.

Note

Both methods adjust operating current assets/liabilities, but only the direct method gives a break-up of cash from operations. Where debentures/loans increase during the year, interest may be computed on the closing balance, or on the opening balance for the full year plus proportionate interest on additions, with a stated assumption. Current investments carrying a value-change risk are treated as investing activities, not cash equivalents. Refer to the full text of AS 3 for complete requirements.

Chapter 12 — Buy-Back of Securities

Source: 87725bos-aps2158-ch12.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Buy-back is a company purchasing its own shares/specified securities; bought-back securities must be cancelled, so buy-back reduces share capital. A company cannot buy its own shares for investment.
  • Objectives/advantages: increase EPS (fewer outstanding shares); increase promoters' holding; discourage hostile takeover bids; support the share price when it is below its worth in a depressed market; and return surplus cash to shareholders.
  • Governed by Sections 68, 69 and 70 of the Companies Act, 2013 (and SEBI (Buy-back of Securities) Regulations for listed companies).

Key Definitions / Terms

Sources of buy-back [Sec 68(1)]
(i) free reserves; (ii) the securities premium account; or (iii) the proceeds of a fresh issue of shares/other specified securities. No buy-back of any kind of security out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind (e.g. buy equity out of preference-issue proceeds).
Free reserves
Reserves that, per the latest audited balance sheet, are available for distribution as dividend (includes securities premium per Explanation II); excludes unrealised/notional gains, revaluation of assets and fair-value changes recognised in equity. Revaluation reserve represents unrealised profit and cannot be used for buy-back.
Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR) [Sec 69]
Where shares are bought out of free reserves/securities premium, a sum equal to the nominal (face) value of shares purchased is transferred to CRR, disclosed in the Balance Sheet. CRR may be applied only to issue fully paid bonus shares — it is not distributable, hence not a free reserve.
Specified securities
Employees' stock options or other securities notified by the Central Government (Explanation I).
Premium on buy-back
Excess of buy-back price over par value — adjusted against securities premium and/or free reserves.

How It Works / Logic

  • Authorisation [Sec 68(2)(a)&(b)]: buy-back must be authorised by the articles and by a special resolution in general meeting. Exception: if buy-back is ≤10% of paid-up equity capital + free reserves, a Board resolution suffices (no special resolution/article authority needed).
  • Three quantitative limits (maximum permissible = least of the three):
TestCondition
Resources Test [68(2)(c)]Total buy-back ≤ 25% of total paid-up capital + free reserves
Shares Outstanding Test [68(2)(d)]Equity bought back in a financial year ≤ 25% of total paid-up equity capital of that year
Debt-Equity Ratio Test [68(2)(e)]Post buy-back debt (secured + unsecured) ≤ 2× (paid-up capital + free reserves), i.e. D/E not more than 2:1
  • Further Sec 68 conditions: all securities bought back must be fully paid-up; listed-company buy-back must comply with SEBI regulations; only one buy-back offer within any period of one year from the closure of a previous offer; every buy-back completed within 12 months of the resolution.
  • Explanatory statement accompanying the special-resolution notice must disclose all material facts, the necessity, the class of security, the amount to be invested and the time limit.
  • Declaration of solvency [68(6)]: filed with the Registrar and SEBI before buy-back, verified by affidavit, signed by at least two directors (one the managing director), stating the company can meet liabilities and will not be rendered insolvent within one year. (Not filed with SEBI by unlisted companies.)
  • Post buy-back obligations: extinguish and physically destroy securities within 7 days of completion; maintain a register of securities bought; file a return with the Registrar/SEBI within 30 days; and make no further issue of the same kind of shares for 6 months (except bonus issue or discharge of subsisting obligations such as conversion of warrants/ESOP/sweat equity/preference/debentures).
  • Penalty [68(10)]: company fine ₹1–3 lakh; every officer in default — imprisonment up to 3 years and/or fine ₹1–3 lakh.
  • Section 70 prohibitions: no buy-back (directly/indirectly) through a subsidiary or through investment companies; nor while a default subsists in repayment of deposits/interest, redemption of debentures/preference shares, payment of dividend, or repayment of term loan/interest (unless remedied and 3 years elapsed); nor if the company has not complied with Sec 92 (annual return), 123 (dividend within 30 days), 127 (failure to distribute dividend) or 129 (financial statements).
  • SEBI regulations (listed): no open-market offer for 15% or more of paid-up capital + free reserves; only one offer within a year; at least 50% of the earmarked amount must be utilised for the buy-back.

Examples

  • Typical journal entries (M Ltd. / Dee Ltd.):
    • Sale of investment to fund buy-back: Bank A/c Dr, Profit & Loss A/c Dr (loss) → Investment A/c.
    • Amount due: Equity share capital A/c Dr + Premium payable on buy-back A/c Dr → Equity shares buy-back A/c.
    • Payment: Equity shares buy-back A/c Dr → Bank A/c.
    • Premium adjustment: Securities premium A/c Dr → Premium payable on buy-back A/c.
    • CRR creation: Revenue/General reserve A/c Dr → Capital Redemption Reserve A/c (equal to nominal value bought back).

Why It Matters / Application

The statutory tests ensure a buy-back does not over-leverage the company or endanger solvency, and the source restrictions protect creditors and prevent buy-back out of unrealised profits. Creating the CRR preserves the company's capital base by substituting the nominal value of the cancelled capital, so the transaction reduces free reserves rather than protected capital.

Note

When buy-back is out of free reserves (or securities premium), CRR equals the nominal value of shares bought back. Because CRR is usable only for bonus shares (not distributable), it must be excluded from "future equity" when applying the Debt-Equity Test — so the maximum permissible buy-back and the CRR amount are solved by simultaneous equations (3x = y where the offer price is ₹30 on a ₹10 par). Partly paid shares can never be bought back; premium on buy-back is met from securities premium and/or free reserves; revaluation reserve cannot be used.

Chapter 13 — Amalgamation of Companies

Source: 87726bos-aps2158-ch13.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Amalgamation is a business-restructuring strategy governed by AS 14, "Accounting for Amalgamations" (read with the Companies Act, 2013). It covers three related activities: Amalgamation (two or more companies wound up, a new company formed to take over their business — at least three companies involved), Absorption (an existing company takes over one or more existing companies — at least two involved, no new company formed), and External Reconstruction (a newly formed company takes over an existing loss-making company to reorganise its financial structure — only two companies).
  • Two parties: the Transferor / Vendor company (whose business is taken over and whose books are closed) and the Transferee / Vendee / Purchasing company (which absorbs the assets and liabilities).
  • AS 14 recognises two types: amalgamation in the nature of merger (a genuine pooling of assets, liabilities, shareholders' interests and businesses) and amalgamation in the nature of purchase (one company acquires another). The type dictates the accounting method — Pooling of Interests for merger, Purchase Method for purchase.

Key Definitions / Terms

Merger — five conditions [AS 14 para 3(e)]
ALL must hold: (i) all assets & liabilities of transferor become those of transferee; (ii) shareholders holding not less than 90% of the face value of equity shares (excluding shares already held by transferee/subsidiaries) become equity shareholders of transferee; (iii) consideration to those agreeing shareholders is discharged wholly by issue of equity shares (cash only for fractional shares); (iv) the transferor's business is intended to be carried on; (v) no adjustment to book values except to achieve uniformity of accounting policies (e.g. restating inventory from weighted-average to FIFO). If even one condition fails, it is amalgamation in the nature of purchase.
Purchase Consideration [AS 14 para 3(g)]
The aggregate of shares and other securities issued and payment made in cash or other assets by the transferee to the shareholders (equity and preference) of the transferor. It excludes amounts paid directly to debenture-holders or creditors — liabilities not taken over are settled by the transferor itself.
Realisation Account
The account opened in the transferor's books to close them — assets (at book value) are debited to it, liabilities taken over are credited, purchase consideration is credited, and the balancing figure is the profit/loss on realisation transferred to Equity Shareholders.
Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve (AAR)
Under the purchase method, a debit reserve created to bring in the transferor's statutory reserves; shown as a separate negative line item under Reserves & Surplus.

How It Works / Logic

  • Computing Purchase Consideration — four methods: (1) Lump-sum — a fixed agreed amount; (2) Net Payments — total of individual payments (cash, shares, debentures) made to equity and preference shareholders; (3) Net Assets — agreed value of assets taken over less outside liabilities taken over (excluding share capital & reserves); where no value is agreed, book values are used; (4) Intrinsic value / Share exchange — shares issued in the ratio of intrinsic values (e.g. transferor share worth ₹20, transferee ₹30 → exchange ratio 20/30) multiplied by intrinsic value. Consideration may be recorded at issue price or par value; probable, reasonably estimable future adjustments are included.
  • Pooling of Interests Method (merger): assets, liabilities AND reserves of the transferor are taken at existing carrying amounts (only adjusted for uniform policies). The difference between the share capital + additional consideration issued by the transferee and the transferor's recorded share capital is adjusted against reserves (Step I = capital & consideration issued; Step II = existing capital of transferor; Step III = I − II adjusted in reserves, usually a deduction). No goodwill/capital reserve arises.
  • Purchase Method (purchase): assets & liabilities recorded at existing carrying amounts OR at fair values. Compare Net Assets (Total assets − outside liabilities) with Purchase Consideration: if Net Assets > PC → Capital Reserve; if PC > Net Assets → Goodwill (amortised over five years unless a longer period is justified). Only statutory reserves of the transferor are carried forward (via the AAR entry below); general reserve and P&L balances are NOT recorded.
  • Statutory reserve entry (purchase method): Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve A/c Dr … To Statutory Reserves. When the statute no longer requires the reserve, both accounts are reversed. AAR cannot be set off against the statutory reserve and is shown separately (negative).
  • Closing the Vendor's books (Realisation A/c): (1) transfer all assets at book value to Realisation A/c (cash excluded if not taken over; P&L Dr. balance and unwritten-off expenses are NOT assets); (2) transfer liabilities taken over to Realisation A/c (provisions split — expected liability transferred, surplus is profit); (3) debit purchasing company & credit Realisation A/c with PC; (4) on receipt, debit cash/shares/debentures received; (5) liquidation expenses — if borne by vendor, Dr Realisation; if by purchaser, ignore or record reimbursement (excess above the cap is borne by vendor via Realisation); (6) pay off liabilities not assumed; (7)–(8) settle preference shareholders (excess over capital → Realisation); (9)–(10) transfer equity capital, reserves & Realisation balance to Equity Shareholders A/c and pay them off.
  • Books of Purchasing company: (1) Business Purchase A/c Dr … To Liquidator of transferor with PC; (2) debit assets (except goodwill) at agreed values, credit liabilities at agreed values and Business Purchase; the balancing difference is Goodwill (Dr) or Capital Reserve (Cr); (3) Liquidator A/c Dr … To Share Capital / Securities Premium / Cash to discharge PC; (4) debentures reissued (e.g. at premium/discount); (5) liquidation expenses paid by purchaser are debited to Goodwill/Capital Reserve.
  • "Entries at par value": to avoid a large Goodwill sitting alongside a large Securities Premium, negotiation may use market value but entries are made at par, so Goodwill/Capital Reserve auto-adjusts for the premium.
  • Inter-company adjustments: Mutual owings — after acquisition entries, pass Trade payables Dr … To Trade receivables. Unrealised profit on stock — profit loaded into stock held by the debtor company must be eliminated by the purchaser (Dr Goodwill/Capital Reserve, Cr Stock), or by reducing stock to cost during acquisition. Inter-company loans — Loan A/c Dr … To Loans & Advances to eliminate.

Why It Matters / Application

Amalgamation accounting determines how the combined entity's assets, liabilities, capital and reserves are presented — and whether goodwill or capital reserve emerges — directly affecting reported net worth and future profits (through goodwill amortisation). Correctly identifying merger versus purchase, computing purchase consideration under the right method, and eliminating inter-company items are essential to produce a true and fair post-amalgamation balance sheet and to comply with AS 14 and the Companies Act, 2013.

Note

Purchase consideration never includes payments to debenture-holders or creditors. Under the purchase method only statutory reserves survive (via the AAR), whereas under pooling all reserves are carried at book value. Preference shareholders are entitled only to their contributed capital unless the scheme provides otherwise, and must bear a loss if funds are insufficient after settling creditors. Unrealised profit on inter-company stock and mutual owings are eliminated only after the normal acquisition entries are passed.

Chapter 14 — Internal Reconstruction

Source: 87727bos-aps2158-ch14.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • Why reconstruct. After years of losses a company's Balance Sheet no longer shows a true and fair view: assets are over-valued, fictitious/unrepresented intangibles and a debit balance of Profit & Loss (carried-forward losses) inflate net worth, so the company is over-capitalised. Reconstruction reorganises affairs by revaluing assets, reassessing liabilities, writing off losses, reducing paid-up value of shares and/or varying rights of share classes — the object being to reorganise capital or compound with creditors so the company is bailed out without winding up.
  • Internal vs External reconstruction. In internal reconstruction the existing company is not liquidated; its capital and debt structure is re-cast to restore normalcy, involving reduction of capital and often sacrifice by outsiders (e.g. debenture holders). Governed by Sec 61 & 66 of the Companies Act 2013; needs Tribunal/court confirmation and legal formalities. In external reconstruction the existing company is liquidated and a new company formed whose shareholders are substantially the same; there is no capital reduction (fresh share capital is raised), no variation of rights, it needs no court interference and is faster. External reconstruction is essentially "amalgamation in the nature of merger" under AS 14 (regulated by Sec 232).
  • Six methods (often used together): alteration of share capital; sub-division & consolidation of shares; conversion of shares into stock or vice-versa; variation of shareholders' rights; reduction of share capital; compromise/arrangement; and surrender of shares.

Key Definitions / Terms

Alteration of Share Capital (Sec 61)
If authorised by Articles, by ordinary resolution in general meeting a limited company may: increase authorised capital; consolidate or sub-divide shares; convert shares into stock or back; or cancel un-taken shares. Does NOT need the Capital Reduction A/c.
Sub-division / Consolidation
Splitting shares into a smaller denomination or combining into a larger one, keeping the paid-up:unpaid proportion unchanged. Entry only transfers within Share Capital, e.g. Equity Share Capital (₹100) A/c Dr. → Equity Share Capital (₹10) A/c.
Conversion into Stock (Sec 61)
Fully-paid shares consolidated into "stock" (a bundle divisible into aliquot parts, transferable in any fraction); entry merely transfers Share Capital A/c to Stock A/c; a Stock Register is maintained. Re-conversion reverses it.
Variation of Rights (Sec 48)
Changing rights of a class (e.g. dividend rate, or cumulative→non-cumulative preference) without changing the amount of capital — entry debits old-class capital, credits new-class capital. No Capital Reduction A/c.
Reduction of Share Capital (Sec 66)
Subject to Tribunal confirmation, by special resolution capital may be reduced by: (a) extinguishing/reducing liability on unpaid amounts; (b) paying off paid-up capital in excess of needs; (c) cancelling paid-up capital lost or unrepresented by assets.
Capital Reduction / Reconstruction Account
A new account crediting the sacrifice made by shareholders (and outsiders) for the lost portion of capital; utilised to write off accumulated losses, fictitious assets and over-valued assets; any final credit balance goes to Capital Reserve.
Surrender of Shares
Shares are sub-divided, shareholders surrender part, surrendered shares are re-issued to debenture holders/creditors (reducing their claims); unutilised surrendered shares are cancelled to Reconstruction A/c.

How It Works / Logic

  • Reducing paid-up value. Reduction only in paid value: Share Capital A/c Dr., To Capital Reduction A/c (the sacrifice). Reduction in both nominal & paid value: debit old Share Capital to close it, credit new Share Capital with the retained paid value and credit Capital Reduction with the difference.
  • Paying off excess capital. Share Capital (old) Dr.; To Share Capital (new) and To Sundry Shareholders; then Sundry Shareholders Dr. To Bank.
  • Compromise/arrangement. Reserves given up: Reserves A/c Dr. To Reconstruction. Creditors accepting less: Outside Liabilities (and any Provision) Dr. To Reconstruction.
  • Standard entry rules. (1) Alteration & variation of rights do NOT open the Capital Reduction A/c. (2) Only reduction of capital, unrepresented reserves, compromise with outsiders and surrender of shares route amounts through Capital Reduction/Reconstruction. (3) An appreciation in an asset or a fall in a liability is credited to Capital Reduction. (4) The P&L debit balance and asset over-valuations are written off by debiting Capital Reduction; reserves may be used too. (5) Any final credit balance → Capital Reserve; if losses/write-offs exceed the reconstruction amount, reserves are adjusted against them.
  • Arrears of preference dividend. On cumulative preference shares arrears are settled per scheme — e.g. part waived and the rest satisfied by issuing equity shares (Capital Reduction A/c Dr. To Equity Share Capital), or arrears simply cancelled.
  • Contingent liabilities. Recorded when the scheme settles them — the settlement paid via Bank, any recovery (e.g. from insurer) credited, and the net loss debited to Reconstruction.
  • Balance Sheet after reconstruction. Add the words "and Reduced" after the company name only if the Court so orders; amounts written off fixed assets under the scheme must be disclosed for five years. Shares issued for consideration other than cash are disclosed.

Why It Matters / Application

Internal reconstruction lets a viable but loss-laden company clean up its Balance Sheet — extinguishing accumulated losses and over-stated assets against the sacrifice of shareholders and creditors — without the cost, delay and disruption of liquidation. It restores a realistic net-worth, improves working capital (fresh cash from rights issues or debentures), and rebalances the debt structure, giving the entity a fresh, financeable start while preserving its legal identity.

Note

Alteration of capital and variation of rights never touch the Capital Reduction/Reconstruction A/c — they are mere re-designations. The account appears only for capital reduction, compromise with outsiders and surrender. Always route asset appreciation/liability fall to its credit, close it by transferring the surplus to Capital Reserve (never distributable as dividend), and remember the "and Reduced" suffix is used only if the Court directs.

Chapter 15 — Accounting for Branches including Foreign Branches

Source: 87728bos-aps2158-ch15.pdf

Core Concept(s)

  • A branch is an establishment at a location different from the Head Office (H.O.) carrying on the same or substantially the same activity. A branch presupposes a H.O. (the principal place of business). Unlike a department (a division at the same location, needing apportionment of common expenses), branch accounts may be kept at branch or H.O., reconciliation is needed for independent branches, and foreign-currency conversion is needed for foreign branches.
  • Classification (accounting view): Inland Branches — (a) Dependent (whole records kept at H.O.) and (b) Independent (maintain own records); plus Foreign Branches.
  • Dependent branch — business, policy and administration wholly controlled by H.O.; accounts maintained by H.O. from returns received. Types: order-booking branch, sale branch with H.O. collecting cash, and retail branch selling H.O.-supplied goods.
  • Methods of charging goods to branches: at cost, at selling price (loading added — greater control; suits tea, petrol, ghee), or at wholesale price (for retail branches).

Key Definitions / Terms

Loading
Difference between invoice/selling price and cost; the unrealised (anticipatory) profit element to be eliminated from unsold branch stock via a Stock Reserve.
Branch Adjustment A/c
Under stock & debtors (invoice price) method — holds the loading; balance = gross profit, transferred to Branch P&L.
Integral Foreign Operation (IFO)
Foreign operation whose activities are an integral extension of the reporting enterprise (e.g. sells imported H.O. goods and remits proceeds).
Non-Integral Foreign Operation (NFO)
Operates substantially independently — accumulates cash/monetary items, earns income, borrows in local currency.
Monetary / Non-monetary items
Monetary = cash, receivables, payables (fixed/determinable money); non-monetary = fixed assets, investments, inventory.
Foreign Currency Translation Reserve (FCTR)
Accumulates NFO exchange differences until disposal of the net investment.

How It Works / Logic

  • Debtors Method (small branches, goods at cost): a single Branch A/c is debited with opening assets (stock, debtors, petty cash, fixed assets, prepaid), goods sent, direct purchases paid by H.O., and expenses paid by H.O.; credited with remittances, returns to H.O. and closing assets. Balancing figure = net profit/loss. Discount/allowances to debtors are NOT separately debited (cash already netted). Accuracy provable via a Memorandum Branch Trading & P&L A/c.
  • Stock & Debtors Method (tighter control): H.O. keeps Branch Stock, Branch Debtors, Branch Expenses, Goods sent to Branch, Branch Cash, Branch Fixed Asset and Branch P&L accounts. Branch Stock is debited with goods sent and credited with cash/credit sales and closing stock — its balance reveals shortage or surplus and (after closing stock) gross profit. Expenses (incl. bad debts, discount) go to Branch Expenses A/c; both stock GP and expenses close into Branch P&L.
  • Final Accounts (Trading & P&L) Method: a memorandum Branch Trading and P&L A/c treating the branch as a separate entity — gives full gross & net profit; entries have no double-entry effect.
  • Goods at selling price: Branch Stock A/c is at selling price; loading is credited to Branch Adjustment A/c when goods sent, debited when returned. Goods lost / pilferage: debit Goods Lost A/c (cost) + Branch Adjustment A/c (loading), credit Branch Stock (theft → Loss by Theft A/c). Discrepancies arise from wrong loading %, off-price sales, allowances, physical loss, or stock-taking errors.
  • Eliminating unrealised profit: on closing stock create Stock Reserve = loading portion (debit Branch Adjustment A/c, credit Stock Reserve). In debtors method under invoice price, opening-stock loading is credited to Branch A/c (Stock Reserve released) and closing-stock loading debited. Stock Reserve is deducted from closing stock in the Balance Sheet so stock shows at cost.
  • Wholesale branch method: H.O. (manufacturer) invoices retail shops at wholesale price (cost + wholesale profit). Manufacturer's profit = wholesale − cost (deemed always earnable); branch profit = shop sales − wholesale price of goods sold. Branch Stock/Trading A/c is debited with opening stock + goods at wholesale price, credited with sales, closing stock and goods lost (all at wholesale price). Stock Reserve = wholesale price − cost on closing stock (charged to H.O. P&L).
  • Independent branch: keeps full double-entry books, opens a Head Office A/c; H.O. opens a Branch A/c (converse of each other). Branch prepares its own trial balance and final accounts and sends copies to H.O. for incorporation. A debit balance in Branch A/c equals net assets at the branch; on consolidation both current accounts are eliminated.
  • Incorporation (2 methods): (I) transfer only branch profit (Branch A/c Dr, To P&L) keeping separate B/S — branch shows H.O. advance as "capital"; (II) transfer each individual revenue, asset and liability balance to close both current accounts, then reopen assets/liabilities/H.O. balance next year. Inter-branch transactions routed through H.O.; branch fixed assets kept in H.O. books → depreciation debited to Branch A/c (branch: Depreciation Dr, To H.O.); H.O. time-cost charged to branch reduces H.O. expense heads.
  • Reconciliation of Branch/H.O. current accounts: disagreement arises from goods-in-transit, cash-in-transit, direct collections/payments by the other party, and expense allocations not yet recorded. Rule: pass the adjusting entry only at the end where recording was omitted. Goods in transit → branch: Goods in Transit A/c Dr, To H.O. Cash in transit → H.O.: Cash in Transit A/c Dr, To Branch. After adjustments the two balances tally.
  • Foreign branch translation (AS 11 revised 2003): foreign branches keep books in local currency; the trial balance is translated before incorporation. See table below.

Why It Matters / Application

Branch accounting lets an enterprise measure the profitability and net investment of each geographically dispersed unit while producing one consolidated set of financial statements. The chosen dependent-branch method trades off simplicity (Debtors) against control (Stock & Debtors) and completeness (Final Accounts); invoicing at loaded prices adds pricing control but forces elimination of unrealised profit so assets are not overstated. For independent and foreign branches, disciplined reconciliation of the reciprocal current accounts and correct AS 11 translation (with exchange differences correctly routed to P&L for integral operations or to the FCTR for non-integral) are essential for a true and fair consolidated position.

Note

Stock Reserve is always computed on the loading in unsold stock and deducted from closing stock in the Balance Sheet so stock is carried at cost. In reconciliation, record the adjusting entry only at the point where the transaction was omitted — never re-record where it is already captured. Under AS 11 the indicators of an NFO (independent financing, low proportion of dealings with H.O., local-currency sales/expenses, active local market) are judgemental, not conclusive; subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures are treated as non-integral. Exchange differences in the FCTR remain there — even after an NFO becomes integral — until the net investment is disposed of.
