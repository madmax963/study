Chapter 1 — Preliminary

Source: Companies Act 2013 — Definitions and Fundamental Concepts

Core Concept(s)

  • Company: An association of persons formed with capital raised in shares by contributing different sums of money. It is registered under the Companies Act, 2013, and is a legal entity separate from its members.
  • Private Company: A company with minimum paid-up capital of ₹1 lakh, restrictions on shares transfer, limited to 200 members, and cannot invite public deposits.
  • Public Company: A company with minimum paid-up capital of ₹5 lakh, free transferability of shares, no restriction on membership, and can invite public deposits.
  • Share: A unit of capital of a company, representing ownership interest. Shares are transferable and carry voting and dividend rights.
  • Memorandum of Association (MoA): The charter document of the company defining its constitution, objects, and the relationship between the company and the outside world.
  • Articles of Association (AoA): Internal regulations of the company governing the conduct of its affairs and the relationship between the company and its members.

Key Definitions / Terms

Company
An association of persons registered under the Companies Act, 2013 with separate legal personality, perpetual succession, and limited liability to the extent of capital contributed.
Shareholder / Member
Person who holds shares in a company and is registered in the register of members. Represents partial ownership in the company.
Director
Person appointed to manage the company's affairs. May be whole-time, part-time or independent. Must hold at least one share of the company.
Promoter
Person who takes initiative in founding and promoting a company. Not a legal entity but acts to bring the company into existence.
Subscriber
Person who signs the Memorandum of Association and becomes the first shareholders of the company.
Paid-up Capital
The amount of capital actually received by the company from the sale of shares. Must meet statutory minimum for company type.

How It Works / Logic

  • Legal Personality Doctrine: A company, once incorporated, becomes a separate legal entity with rights and liabilities distinct from those of its members. This stems from the principle established in Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd — the company can own property, sue and be sued in its own name, and is responsible for its own debts.
  • Limited Liability: Shareholders' liability is limited to the amount of their investment (paid-up capital). They cannot be called upon to pay the company's debts from personal funds beyond their share capital contribution.
  • Capital Formation: A private company requires minimum paid-up capital of ₹1 lakh; public companies require ₹5 lakh. This protects creditors by ensuring minimum assets.
  • Constitutional Documents: The MoA defines the company's outer limits (objects, powers, capital); AoA governs internal management. Both must be registered with the Registrar of Companies.

Examples

  • Company Formation: Five persons come together and decide to start an engineering business. They register as a private company under the Companies Act with ₹5,00,000 capital. The company becomes a distinct entity; the members' personal assets are protected from company creditors (limited liability).
  • Share Transfer: In a public company, if a shareholder wants to sell their 100 shares, they can transfer them freely (transferability). The new holder becomes the member; the company's management structure remains unchanged.

Why It Matters / Application

Understanding the fundamental nature of a company and its constitutional documents is essential to comprehend all subsequent provisions of company law. The concept of separate legal personality, limited liability, and the supremacy of MoA and AoA underpin the entire regulatory framework.

Note

The Companies Act 2013 modernizes corporate law in India. The distinction between private and public companies is critical in determining applicable regulations — many chapters apply differently to each type.

Chapter 2 — Incorporation of Company

Source: Companies Act 2013 — Sections 7-15 on Company Registration

Core Concept(s)

  • Incorporation Process: The formal procedure by which a company is brought into legal existence by registration under the Companies Act 2013. Registration is conclusive proof of incorporation.
  • Registrar of Companies (RoC): Government office responsible for registration, regulation, and administration of companies under the Act. Every state has an RoC.
  • Certificate of Incorporation (CoI): Formal document issued by RoC confirming that a company has been registered and has started its corporate existence.
  • Pre-incorporation Contracts: Contracts entered into before incorporation by promoters on behalf of the company to be formed. The company is not bound by these until it expressly adopts them.

Key Definitions / Terms

Incorporation
Process of registering a company under the Companies Act to give it legal status and bring it into existence as a separate legal entity.
Certificate of Incorporation
Document issued by RoC confirming that a company has been duly registered and has commenced its corporate existence.
Registrar of Companies
Government official appointed under the Act responsible for registering companies and monitoring compliance with statutory requirements.
Pre-incorporation Contract
Agreement entered into prior to incorporation of a company, typically by promoters. Company is not bound unless it expressly adopts the contract post-incorporation.
Registered Office
Principal office of the company as stated in its MoA, where records are kept and official communications are received.

How It Works / Logic

  • Incorporation Procedure: Promoters prepare MoA and AoA → Signed by all subscribers → Applications submitted to RoC → RoC verifies documents and compliance → Issues Certificate of Incorporation.
  • Effect of Incorporation: From the date of incorporation, the company becomes a distinct legal entity, separate from its promoters and members. It can own property, sue and be sued, and enter contracts in its own name.
  • Pre-incorporation Contracts: As the company did not exist before incorporation, it cannot be a party to contracts made before its formation. Such contracts may be:
    • Ratified (expressly adopted) by the company post-incorporation — the company then becomes bound.
    • Novated — old contract withdrawn and new contract entered into with the company.
    • Left unenforceable — if not adopted, promoters remain liable unless released by the other party.

Examples

  • Incorporation Process: Mr. A and three others decide to start a trading company. They prepare the Memorandum and Articles, sign them, and send the application to the RoC. After verification, RoC issues a Certificate of Incorporation. The company now legally exists.
  • Pre-incorporation Contract: Before the company was registered, promoters signed a lease for office space in the company's name. The company, once incorporated, can ratify this lease and become bound by it. Without ratification, the promoters remain liable.

Why It Matters / Application

Incorporation is the threshold requirement — nothing in company law applies until a company is incorporated. Understanding incorporation procedures is fundamental for anyone involved in starting a company. The doctrine on pre-incorporation contracts clarifies that the company and its promoters are distinct legal entities.

Chapter 3 — Prospectus and Allotment of Securities

Source: Companies Act 2013 — Sections 23-32 on Prospectus and Offer Documents

Core Concept(s)

  • Prospectus: A document issued by a company inviting the public to subscribe for its shares or debentures. Contains full details of the company's business, financial position, and risk factors.
  • Contents of Prospectus: Must include details of directors, auditors, capital structure, financial performance, risk factors, and any material contracts entered into by the company.
  • Allotment: The process of distributing shares or debentures to applicants. Allotment creates a binding contract between the company and the allottee.
  • Liability for Prospectus: Directors, promoters, and others responsible for preparing the prospectus are liable for any misstatements or material omissions that mislead investors.

Key Definitions / Terms

Prospectus
Document inviting public to invest in company securities; must be registered with SEBI and filed with Registrar before issue.
Offer Document
Document issued in place of prospectus in certain circumstances, e.g., in right issues or bonus issues.
Allotment
Process of distributing shares or debentures to successful applicants; creates contractual relationship.
Allottee
Person to whom shares or debentures are allotted. Allottee is entitled to receive share certificate.
Statement in Lieu of Prospectus
Document filed with RoC by private companies instead of prospectus, containing material information about the company.

How It Works / Logic

  • Regulation of Prospectus: A prospectus must:
    • Be registered with SEBI (for public issues)
    • Be filed with RoC before issue
    • Contain prescribed information regarding directors, capital, business, financials, and risk factors
    • Be signed by directors, promoters, and auditors in prescribed manner
  • Allotment Rules: Allotment must be made on a fair basis when applications exceed availability. For listed companies, SEBI has prescribed allotment procedures (e.g., proportional allotment, lottery method).
  • Misstatement Liability: Any person responsible for a prospectus who makes an untrue statement or material omission is liable to compensate persons who suffer loss, unless they can prove due diligence or reasonable grounds to believe the statement was true.

Examples

  • Prospectus Issue: A pharmaceutical company decides to float an IPO (Initial Public Offering). It prepares a prospectus detailing its business, pipeline of drugs, competitive position, and financial forecasts. The prospectus is registered with SEBI and filed with RoC before the public offer opens.
  • Allotment Process: The IPO receives 10 lakh applications for 2 lakh shares at ₹100 per share. Allotment is made using lottery method — some applicants get allotted, some get partial allotment, and others get no allotment. All allottees receive share certificates.
  • Misstatement Liability: If the prospectus falsely states that the company has FDA approval for a key drug when it does not, investors who subscribe relying on this statement can claim compensation from the company and responsible persons.

Why It Matters / Application

Prospectus regulations protect investing public from fraudulent or misleading information. Allotment procedures ensure fairness and transparency. Understanding these provisions is critical for companies raising funds and for investors evaluating investment opportunities.

Chapter 4 — Share Capital and Debentures

Source: Companies Act 2013 — Sections 43-71 on Capital and Securities

Core Concept(s)

  • Share Capital: The amount of capital raised by the company through issuance of shares. Shares represent the ownership interests of shareholders in the company.
  • Authorized Capital: Maximum amount of capital that a company is authorized to raise as per its MoA. Cannot be exceeded without amending the MoA.
  • Issued Capital: Amount of capital actually issued to members. Cannot exceed authorized capital.
  • Paid-up Capital: Amount actually paid by shareholders on issued shares. May be less than issued capital if shares are partly paid.
  • Debentures: Debt securities issued by a company acknowledging its indebtedness to the holder. Carry fixed interest and a maturity date.
  • Redemption of Debentures: Process of repaying debenture holders on maturity. Company must maintain Debenture Redemption Reserve.

Key Definitions / Terms

Share
Unit of capital of a company, representing proportionate ownership. Carries voting and dividend rights as per Articles.
Equity Share
Share carrying voting rights and entitled to dividend only after meeting all other obligations.
Preference Share
Share with preferential rights to dividend and/or return of capital on winding up.
Debenture
Debt security acknowledging company's indebtedness; carries fixed interest rate and maturity date; secured or unsecured.
Share Certificate
Document issued to shareholder evidencing ownership of shares; is not a negotiable instrument.
Share Transfer
Process of transferring ownership of shares from one person to another; must be registered with company.

How It Works / Logic

  • Capital Structure: The relationship is: Authorized Capital ≥ Issued Capital ≥ Paid-up Capital. A company can issue new shares up to authorized capital, and can increase authorized capital by amending MoA.
  • Share Transfer Rules: When a member wants to transfer shares:
    • Transferor and transferee execute a transfer deed
    • Transfer deed is lodged with the company
    • Company verifies and registers the transfer
    • Transferee becomes the new member upon registration
    • Company may decline transfer if prescribed conditions are met (e.g., private company restrictions)
  • Debenture Redemption: Companies must set aside funds in a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) for redeeming debentures. The DRR must contain at least a specified percentage of the value of debentures issued.

Examples

  • Capital Structure: A company's MoA authorizes capital of ₹10 crores. The Board issues ₹6 crores in shares. Shareholders pay ₹4 crores upfront. Here, Authorized = ₹10 cr, Issued = ₹6 cr, Paid-up = ₹4 cr. Later, if shareholders pay the remaining ₹2 cr, Paid-up becomes ₹6 cr.
  • Debenture Issuance: A company issues ₹50 lakh of 10-year debentures at 9% interest. The company must build a Debenture Redemption Reserve to ensure funds are available to repay debenture holders after 10 years.
  • Share Transfer: Shareholder A holds 500 shares and wants to sell to Shareholder B for ₹50,000. A and B execute a transfer deed; B deposits it with the company; company verifies A's title and B's eligibility; transfer is registered; B becomes the new owner.

Why It Matters / Application

The capital structure determines a company's financing. Understanding the distinction between authorized, issued, and paid-up capital is essential for financial analysis. Share transfer and redemption procedures affect the company's equity base and debt obligations.

Chapter 5 — Deposits

Source: Companies Act 2013 — Chapter V on Company Deposits

Core Concept(s)

  • Deposit: Any borrowing by a company from its members or public (other than banks and financial institutions), repayable with or without interest, within a specified period exceeding 3 months.
  • Purpose: Deposits are a source of short to medium-term finance. Strict regulations govern acceptance, repayment, and disclosure to protect depositors.
  • Prohibition on Deposits: Banking companies and financial institutions cannot accept deposits; deposit-accepting companies must get Board and shareholder approval.

Key Definitions / Terms

Deposit
Money borrowed by company from members or public, repayable with or without interest, within specified period exceeding 3 months.
Secured Deposit
Deposit backed by mortgage or charge on company's immovable properties.
Unsecured Deposit
Deposit not backed by any security, dependent on company's creditworthiness.
Deposit Repayment Default
Failure by company to repay deposit on due date; attracts penalties and liability.

How It Works / Logic

  • Rules for Accepting Deposits: A company can accept deposits only if:
    • Board of Directors approves the proposal
    • Shareholders approve by ordinary resolution
    • Company has net working capital and past profitability as prescribed
    • Company maintains Deposit Repayment Reserve (DRR)
    • Proper disclosure is made in financial statements
  • Deposit Repayment Reserve: Companies must set aside funds equivalent to a percentage of outstanding deposits to ensure repayment capacity.
  • Repayment Guarantee: If a company defaults in repaying deposits, investors can approach the National Consumers Redressal Commission for relief.

Examples

  • Deposit Acceptance: A manufacturing company wants to raise ₹1 crore through deposits. It obtains Board approval, then shareholder approval. It fixes interest at 8% p.a. and repayment term at 3 years. Depositors get deposit certificates and company maintains a reserve fund for repayment.

Why It Matters / Application

Deposits are an important source of finance, especially for SMEs. Strict regulations protect depositors from fraudulent schemes. Understanding deposit rules is critical for both companies seeking to raise deposits and individuals considering deposit investments.

Chapter 13 — General Clauses Act, 1897

Source: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — Foundational Legislation for Statutory Interpretation

Core Concept(s)

  • General Clauses Act: The GCA is a foundational Act that provides standard definitions and rules for interpreting all other Central Acts (except where explicitly excluded).
  • Purpose: To avoid repetition and ensure consistency across all Acts by providing common definitions, rules of interpretation, and powers.
  • Application: Applies to all Central Acts unless expressly or by necessary implication excluded.

Key Definitions / Terms

Person
Includes any company, association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not.
Crown
The Federal Government in the case of federal laws; includes the State Government for state laws.
Oath
Includes affirmation and declaration; applies to non-religious persons.
Subordinate Legislation
Rules, bye-laws, regulations, and notifications issued under statutory authority.

How It Works / Logic

  • Definitions Provided: The GCA provides standard definitions such as "person," "month," "year," "holiday," etc., which apply to all Acts unless otherwise provided.
  • Interpretation Rules: Provides rules for reading Acts, such as:
    • Marginal notes and headings are not part of the law (use only for reference)
    • Words used in prior Acts with defined meanings should be interpreted consistently
    • Gender and number are interchangeable
  • Powers and Procedures: Defines standard procedures for exercising powers under Acts, such as when rule-making authorities can delegate powers, how notices must be published, etc.

Why It Matters / Application

The GCA is the lexicon of Indian legislation. A working knowledge of the GCA is essential for anyone reading or interpreting any Central Act, as it provides the default meanings and rules. It ensures consistency across the legal system.

Chapter 14 — Interpretation of Statutes

Source: Principles of Statutory Interpretation under Indian Law

Core Concept(s)

  • Statutory Interpretation: The process of determining the meaning and scope of words and phrases in legislation to apply them to given facts and circumstances.
  • Rules of Interpretation: Courts follow established rules (literal, golden, mischief, purposive) to ascertain legislative intent.
  • Principle of Legality: Laws must be clear and unambiguous; any ambiguity is interpreted against the drafter (Crown) for protection of the subject.

Key Definitions / Terms

Literal Rule
Words are interpreted in their ordinary, plain meaning, without reading in anything not stated.
Golden Rule
When literal meaning produces an absurdity, modify interpretation to avoid absurdity, keeping as close as possible to literal meaning.
Mischief Rule
Look to the mischief or evil the Act sought to remedy and interpret words to suppress that mischief.
Purposive Rule
Interpret words in light of the object and purpose of the Act.

How It Works / Logic

  • Hierarchy of Rules: Courts typically follow the literal rule first. If it leads to absurdity or contradiction, they apply the golden rule. If the Act's purpose is unclear from the text, the mischief or purposive rule is used.
  • Context Matters: Courts consider the historical context, earlier legislation on the subject, and related provisions in the same Act to determine meaning.
  • Presumptions: Courts presume:
    • Parliament did not intend to change the law (so amendments are narrowly construed)
    • Parliament intended consistent usage of terms (if a word is defined in one section, it has the same meaning throughout)
    • Parliament did not intend to bind the Crown or exclude judicial review

Examples

  • Literal vs. Golden Rule: An Act states "No vehicle shall be brought into the park." Literally, this includes ambulances and fire engines (vehicles). But applying the golden rule, we interpret it to exclude emergency vehicles because the mischief was to prevent traffic congestion, not to block emergency services.
  • Mischief Rule: The Act was passed to curb unfair contract terms. When interpreting a provision on "reasonable terms," courts look to the mischief of unfairness and interpret "reasonable" to mean "fair and non-exploitative."

Why It Matters / Application

Statutory interpretation is central to legal practice. Courts' interpretation of legislative text determines how laws apply in practice. Understanding the rules and principles helps predict how courts will likely interpret a statute in a given situation.

Module 1 — Introduction to Intellectual Property

Source: Intellectual Property Law — Foundations

Core Concept(s)

  • Intellectual Property (IP): Creations of the mind — inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, symbols, names, and images — that are protected in law, giving creators exclusive rights to use and profit from their creations.
  • Categories of IP: Patents (inventions), Copyrights (literary/artistic works), Trademarks (signs/symbols), Designs (shape/appearance), Trade Secrets (confidential business information).
  • IP Rights: Exclusive rights granted to creators enabling them to control use and derive economic benefit from their creations for a limited period.
  • Public Domain: Knowledge and works not protected by IP rights, freely available for use by all.

Key Definitions / Terms

Patent
Exclusive right granted to an inventor for an invention (product or process), allowing no one else to make, use, or sell the invention without permission.
Copyright
Exclusive right in literary, dramatic, musical, artistic works, and cinematograph films; automatically granted upon creation.
Trademark
Sign (word, symbol, design, etc.) used to distinguish goods/services of one person from those of another; protects reputation and consumer goodwill.
Trade Secret
Confidential business information giving competitive advantage; protected by confidentiality agreements.
Design
Visual appearance (shape, color, pattern) of an article; protects aesthetic aspects distinct from functional aspects.

How It Works / Logic

  • Purpose of IP Protection: To incentivize innovation and creativity by allowing creators to recover investment and profit from creations, while eventually allowing ideas to enter public domain for societal benefit.
  • Balance: IP law balances creators' rights (monopoly) against public interest (access, competition, follow-on innovation). Protection is limited in duration and scope.
  • Acquisition of IP Rights:
    • Patents: Require filing an application with examination and grant process
    • Copyrights: Automatic upon creation; registration optional but advisable
    • Trademarks: Require registration; rights can be lost if not used or if mark becomes generic
    • Designs: Require registration; protection limited to aesthetic aspects

Examples

  • Patent: A researcher discovers a new drug molecule effective against a disease. Filing a patent application gives the researcher exclusive rights for 20 years to make, use, and sell the drug. After 20 years, the patent expires and others can manufacture generics.
  • Copyright: An author writes a novel. Upon publication, copyright automatically protects the work for the author's lifetime plus 60 years. The author can control reproduction, translation, and adaptation.
  • Trademark: A company creates the brand name "TechWave" for its software products. Registration as a trademark prevents competitors from using similar marks, protecting the company's reputation and customer goodwill.

Why It Matters / Application

IP protection is critical for innovation-driven economies. It incentivizes creators and investors to develop new technologies and creative works. Understanding IP basics is essential for businesses, inventors, and creatives to protect their innovations and respect others' rights.
